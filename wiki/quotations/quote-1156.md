## Rationale

From 2001–2004, steadily cheaper commodity compute power rapidly expanded use of clusters and high-performance computing in military simulation, intelligence analysis, and command-and-control systems, validating the claim of military applications. In the same period, medical and life-sciences research leaned heavily on inexpensive compute for genomics (including completion of the Human Genome Project in 2003), molecular modeling, and medical imaging, while Beowulf clusters and early grid/distributed computing spread across many scientific disciplines. The statement is broad and not tied to a specific technology or deployment metric, but its core assertion—that falling compute costs would matter across military, medical, and scientific domains—was directionally and demonstrably correct within three years and has only become more true since.
