## Rationale

The narrow technical-economic point that server consolidation on higher-performance sockets could save money under per-processor database licensing was valid and did influence some Itanium and other high-end RISC/EPIC deployments in the mid‑2000s. However, within roughly three years x86‑64 Xeon and Opteron rapidly closed and then surpassed Itanium’s price/performance and became the dominant platforms for 64‑bit database servers, while major vendors and ISVs began adjusting licensing metrics toward cores and other factors, so the specific advantage of a two‑way Itanium over four‑way Xeon did not hold broadly. Over the longer term, Itanium remained a niche architecture with small market share and was ultimately discontinued, whereas the general idea of consolidation to reduce per‑socket licensing thrived mainly on x86‑64 platforms rather than on Itanium.
