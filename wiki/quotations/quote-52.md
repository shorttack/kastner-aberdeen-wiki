## Quote

> great PR, but eventually, it's quite possible that corporations will have to sort out their own toxic waste from ordinary office trash,'' said Peter Kastner, a senior analyst at Aberdeen Group in Boston.

## Rationale

From the mid-1990s through the 2000s, corporations increasingly had to segregate hazardous materials like nickel-cadmium batteries, fluorescent lamps, and toner/ink cartridges from ordinary office trash due to RCRA universal-waste rules in the U.S. and WEEE/RoHS in Europe, alongside OEM take-back schemes such as HP’s Planet Partners. Large enterprises commonly implemented separate collection bins and internal policies for these toxic or special wastes, but the practical burden was often shared with vendors and recyclers rather than every corporation fully managing all toxic sorting themselves, so the direction of the prediction was largely correct even if the extent and mechanisms differed somewhat.
