## Quote

> y, it's quite possible that corporations will have to sort out their own toxic waste from ordinary office trash,'' said Peter Kastner, a senior analyst at Aberdeen Group in Boston.

## Rationale

From the mid‑1990s through the early 2000s, regulations such as the U.S. Universal Waste Rule and the 1996 Mercury‑Containing and Rechargeable Battery Management Act, along with the EU’s emerging WEEE and battery directives, pushed organizations to separate batteries and other hazardous office waste from regular trash. Large enterprises increasingly deployed internal programs and policies to collect nickel‑cadmium batteries, toner cartridges, and other e‑waste for vendor take‑back and specialized recyclers, so employees could not simply toss these items into ordinary bins. However, this behavior was far from universal across all corporations by 2002, and separation was often managed through centralized facilities and third‑party services rather than every corporation comprehensively sorting all toxic waste on its own, making the direction of his prediction largely correct but somewhat overstated in scope and speed.
