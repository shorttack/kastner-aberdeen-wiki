## Quote

> a 50-MHz chip should not be that much faster,'' said Peter Kastner, a vice president at market research firm Aberdeen Group in Boston.

## Rationale

In practice, the 486DX2-66 delivered only a modest real-world performance gain over the 486DX-50, often on the order of around 10–20% in typical office and productivity workloads rather than a dramatic leap, so users did not see a transformative improvement from 50 MHz to 66 MHz alone. Benchmarks and contemporary reviews from the early 1990s show that while the DX2-66 could lead in CPU-bound synthetic tests, its advantage was frequently constrained by the shared 33 MHz external bus and memory subsystem, confirming that memory and I/O bottlenecks limited the benefits of the higher internal clock. Over the subsequent few years, the real ‘quantum’ performance jumps instead came from architectural shifts and later Pentium-class CPUs rather than small clock bumps within the 486 line, aligning with Kastner’s implication that this was an incremental, not revolutionary, upgrade.
