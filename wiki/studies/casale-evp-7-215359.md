# The March Quarter Blues - Or, Were We Expecting Too Much, Too Soon?

> EVP #7 analyzes the structural and cyclical reasons why March quarters systematically disappoint investors in high-tech stocks, identifying four business-cycle factors (customer budget timing, new-year caution, informal industry institutionalization, and psychological reset) compounded by the worst recession in fifty years and the resulting Wall Street euphoria that inflated P/E multiples beyond what earnings could justify. Casale argues that instant margin expansion post-recession is structurally impossible and that some companies have already snapped from the strain of maintaining earnings to justify lofty multiples.

**Author:** C.T. Casale · **Date:** 1983-04-05 · **Type:** market-study
**Importance:** high — *Earliest known systematic articulation of the March quarter seasonal pattern in high-tech investment research; written in real time as the first post-recession recovery earnings season unfolded in spring 1983, making it a primary source document for this inflection point.*
**Prescience:** high — *Casale correctly predicted that 'a raging bull leg' had led investors to expect too much too soon: the 1983 tech sector experienced exactly the P/E compression and earnings disappointment cycle he described, setting the template for subsequent tech corrections.*

## Key observations (top 25)

- **1983** — Wall Street behavior: earnings estimate inflation: Earnings estimates scaled up by pennies/nickels/dimes to justify lofty P/E multiples when numerator (stock price) in runaway bull market
- **1983** — March quarter seasonality factor 1: customer budget timing: Major customers on calendar year; Q1 is breather after year-end close. January nullity; February signs of life; March bookings hopefully strong.
- **1983** — March quarter seasonality factor 2: new budget caution: New spending budgets not turned on full force in Q1; period of reflection and re-examination; 'squirrel away a penny or two' mentality
- **1983** — March quarter seasonality factor 3: institutional behavior: Informal institutionalization of modest March quarter; annual sales conferences, vacations for top performers (who bring 70-80% of business); planning conferences
- **1983** — March quarter seasonality factor 4: psychological reset: New Year's Resolution effect: buyers and sellers urge to 'think it through' rather than leap onto quarterly treadmill to keep analysts at bay
- **1982** — Severity of 1981-1982 recession: Worst recession in fifty years; stretched all companies to limit and some beyond
- **1983** — High tech sector recession response: High Tech America did outstanding job: hunkered down, invested for future (depressing near-term margins), prepared for recovery
- **1983** — Investor logic error: post-recession snap-back expectation: Logic: if tech firms did well in recession, recovery will bring massive sales increases and margin expansion. Casale calls this investor logic 'inevitible'
- **1983** — Margin bounceback barrier 1: deferred maintenance/investment: During deep recession firms cut discretionary spend then hit bone; strong pressure to restore investments before competitors gain ground keeps margins low
- **1983** — Margin bounceback barrier 2: need for 20%+ revenue growth: 20%+ revenue growth is minimum for high-tech group; a year's incremental gains can be squeezed from existing headcount but penalizes future; new hires cost money
- **1983** — Margin bounceback barrier 3: headcount and career progression: Career plans in tech assume onward/upward progression; static headcount hurts morale; new hires and support cost money
- **1983** — Margin bounceback barrier 4: competitive parity in depression: If all competitors in same depressed earnings boat, rational to do right for long term; invest now as long as enough business covers costs and modest margins
- **1983** — Earnings cord-snapping incidents: A few companies have snapped too-tightly-stretched cord pursuing quarterly earnings needed to justify lofty multiples; stock prices punished for missing supposedly discounted calendar 1983
- **1983** — Short-termism of investors: Stock punished for missing March quarter that was 'supposedly discounted' into price; confirms industry's comments about short-term nature of investors
