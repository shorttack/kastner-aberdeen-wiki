# Electronic Commerce Professional Services: 1998 Practice Summary

> A 1998 practice summary analyzing the market for professional services supporting electronic commerce deployment. The report defines four categories of EC professional services providers (OEMs, SSPs, IPSPs, EDI/VAN integrators), identifies key implementation challenges (skills shortages, integration complexity, standards immaturity), and profiles 11 major service providers including IBM, HP, Digital, Microsoft, Oracle, SAP, Cambridge Technology Partners, EDS, GEIS, Sterling Commerce, and Harbinger. Key Aberdeen finding: enterprises are turning to outside service providers because internal IS resources lack EC skills.

**Author:** Aberdeen Group · **Date:** 1998-05-01 · **Type:** other-research
**Importance:** medium — *Documents the emergence of e-commerce professional services as a distinct market category in 1998; provides the first structured taxonomy of EC services providers; based on IS manager interviews making it a primary source on enterprise EC adoption drivers.*
**Prescience:** high — *Key predictions verified: EDI/VAN providers were eclipsed by internet commerce; ERP providers (SAP Oracle) proved to be the best entry point to enterprise EC; skills shortage persisted and worsened; B2B internet commerce growth far exceeded forecasts.*

## Key observations (top 25)

- **1998** — Web commerce volume forecast trajectory: $2.6 billion in 1996 to $220+ billion by 2001
- **1998** — B2B internet commerce growth forecast: $8 billion in 1997 to $327 billion in 2002
- **1998** — ERP data availability within enterprises: Less than 15% of users within an organization
- **1998** — Sub-$1000 PC home penetration context: PC home penetration still less than 50%
- **1998** — E-commerce professional services market dynamic: IS decision-makers receive hundreds of calls yearly from professional services providers; it is a buyers market
- **1998** — E-commerce professional services consulting billing rates: $2000 to $5000 per day per consultant for strategy and process reengineering; $750 to $3000 per day for implementation and support
- **1998** — EC systems design maturity trajectory: One to two years away from one-stop shop EC implementation; today individual suppliers provide 80-90% of required functionality
- **1998** — Key EC implementation obstacles listed: Product immaturity; inexperience at professional services level; lack of standards; legacy integration issues; fear of disclosure; cost barriers
- **1998** — EC lifecycle management expectation: E-commerce systems should be considered to last at best only a few years; highly dynamic entity
- **1998** — EDI-origin enterprise advantage in internet EC: Enterprises with EDI origin generally quicker to understand internet EC due to legacy/systems integration experience
- **1998** — EC professional services skill requirements ranked: Infrastructure skills foremost; then architectural design; then installation; then integration/customization; then EDI/security/benchmarking
- **1998** — E-commerce adoption driver: first-mover advantage: He who lingers is lost; companies hesitating to adopt full-blown EC strategy may find themselves trailing competitors
- **1998** — EDI/VAN vs internet EC outcome: Internet threatens to outflank highly profitable VANs; EDI/VAN integrators must embrace internet to survive
- **1998** — ERP as best enterprise EC entry point: ERP providers have the best way in to the enterprise for EC services
- **1998** — IBM Global Services EC staffing: 120000 professionals in Global Services; 10000 dedicated to E-business
- **1998** — HP professional services revenue: $6.2 billion
- **1998** — Digital Equipment professional services revenue: $5.4 billion; 40% of company revenue
- **1998** — Oracle professional services scale: 10000+ consultants in 55 countries; $5.6B in sales
- **1998** — SAP R/3 installation base: Running in 7000+ companies worldwide including Microsoft IBM and General Motors
- **1998** — SAP Pandesic joint venture: Joint venture with Intel (Pandesic LLC) announced August 1997 to bring SAP applications to mid-range and small enterprises
- **1998** — CTP internet-related business share: 70% of new business internet-related in 1997; up from 50% in 1996
- **1998** — EDS E-commerce revenue share: E-commerce only 2% of revenues in 1997
- **1998** — GEIS E-commerce dedicated staff: 2000 service professionals dedicated to E-commerce; only IBM claims more
- **1998** — Sterling Commerce EC positioning: Number two EDI/VAN integrator; most progressive in moving to Internet/EDI; second in size only to GEIS
- **1998** — Harbinger employee count and market position: Fewer than 1000 employees; baby among big three EDI/VAN integrators; lead in PC-based EDI
