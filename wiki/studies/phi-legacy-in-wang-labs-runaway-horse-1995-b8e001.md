# PHI Legacy in Wang Laboratories: Charles Kenney's 'Riding the Runaway Horse' — Chapter 4 'I Have to Do This to Save My Company' (1995)

> Excerpt from Charles C. Kenney's 1992-1995 book 'Riding the Runaway Horse: The Rise and Decline of Wang Laboratories' (Little, Brown). Documents PHI Computer Services as 'one of the best' early-1960s software pioneers; describes Wang Laboratories' 1968 PHI acquisition; identifies Bob Siegel, Harold Koplow, Dave Moros, and Bob Kolk — three of whom came from PHI — as the four men whose products yielded 'billions of dollars of revenues for Wang Laboratories' (Wang 2200 small-business computer, Wang Word Processing System, Wang VS commercial mini). Argues Wang's autobiography 'Lessons' significantly understates the PHI contribution to Wang's success.

## Key observations (top 25)

- **1958** — PHI founding in software-pioneer context: Among Cambridge-area software pioneers, 'Philip Hankins, Inc., was one of the best'
- **1965** — PHI client list: PHI's client list was a 'Who's Who of technology companies and government installations, from the consulting firm Arthur D. Little to NASA and IBM'
- **1965** — PHI saved IBM unveiling: PHI engineers fixed an IBM machine days before unveiling, working nonstop for four days at PHI's ramshackle offices
- **1965** — PHI 360 lease: Hankins expanded by leasing IBM 360 mainframe; 24/7 demand for time
- **1965** — Marine Midland payroll breakthrough: PHI's business 'truly took off' when designers created software enabling a computer to do entire payroll for Marine Midland Bank in NY
- **1965** — Cullinane's commercial vision: Young PHI salesman John Cullinane saw commercial potential; left PHI to found Cullinet, 'at one time, the most successful company of its kind in the world'
- **1965** — Magic atmosphere: Bob Plachta: 'PHI was probably the most fantastic business atmosphere I had ever seen. It was almost like magic.'
- **1968** — Wang acquires PHI: Wang Labs acquired PHI in 1968
- **1995** — Hankins refutation of Lessons: Hankins recalls only one major player left after about a year, and he himself left after about a year and a half — disputing An Wang's 'Lessons' claim that 'a large number' departed
- **1986** — Lessons book underplays PHI: An Wang's 'Lessons' significantly understates PHI contribution; 'crucial point' is that Wang Labs embraced three engineers (Moros, Siegel, Kolk) whose products earned 'many billions of dollars for Wang'
- **1968** — Counterfactual claim: 'Without the manpower that PHI brought to the company, Wang Laboratories might never have been anything more than a small maker of scientific calculators'
- **1975** — Moros & Koplow word processing: An Wang dispatched Koplow and Moros to do something with the company's word processing mess; Moros joined PHI 1964 working on Apollo/Gemini
- **1977** — Siegel-led VS launch: Bob Siegel guided Wang VS development; debuted October 1977; modeled after IBM 360; 'one of the first minis for commercial application'
- **1973** — Kolk-led 2200 development: Bob Kolk guided Wang 2200 development; MIT '58, joined PHI 1966 from North American Aviation/Rockwell
- **1976** — Koplow led WPS development: Harold Koplow led the word-processing group; product became 'industry phenomenon' giving Wang its third hot product when it fully hit market 1979
- **1979** — CEO time allocation: Until ~1981, An Wang spent 65% of time on development projects, 20% on corporate matters (financing/marketing/sales), 15% on customer/employee functions
- **1977** — VS vs VAX timing: Wang VS announced October 1977 — DEC announced VAX 'only weeks after' VS debut
- **1979** — VS vs VAX commercial fit: 'For business needs, the VS proved vastly superior to the VAX'
- **1979** — Wang Datamation ranking: From 1976 (not in top 50 Datamation) to 1979 (#25) to 1981 (#11)
- **1983** — Wang stock 1976-1983: Wang stock effective price climbed from $6.75 (1976) to $800 (1983) — a $8,000 1976 investment became $1M
- **1981** — Cunningham declines Prime: Cunningham, age 38, was offered Prime Computer presidency 'in the fall of 1981'; turned it down twice despite 'sweetened offer' due to confidence in Wang relationship
- **1978** — Wang Super Bowl ad: Wang ran 1978 Super Bowl pregame ad — 30 seconds for $150,000 ($5,000/sec); ran as the very last spot before game; unaided awareness rose 3% to 14%; aided 10% to 35%
- **1979** — Fisher inquiries: Prime CEO Ken Fisher repeatedly asked Cunningham about Wang TV advertising; Cunningham 'innocently' said it didn't work
- **1986** — Wang nepotism: An Wang declared his sons' 'obligation — rather than their privilege' to enter the business; contrasts with DEC's Ken Olsen who rejected nepotism
- **1962** — Warner & Swasey 25%: Warner & Swasey bought 25% Wang Labs interest in early 1960s on Brooke's introduction; gave Wang equity to expand and guidance to manufacture own products
