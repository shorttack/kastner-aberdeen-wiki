# Surfing the Parallel Architectures

> This board-level presentation surveys the 1994 commercial parallel-computing market across uniprocessors, SMP systems, clusters, and massively parallel machines, then compares the leading hardware and database suppliers against Tandem's position. Kastner argues that software-enabled clustering and parallel databases will matter more than pure MPP experimentation, and he frames data warehousing plus distributed object computing as Tandem's best near-term strategic opening. The deck combines architecture taxonomy, vendor-by-vendor competitive critique, and explicit forward-looking judgments about how the market would evolve.


_Published 1994, author **Peter S. Kastner**, type **market-study**._


## Top observations

- Unisys offers adequate clustered OLTP today, but its recent MPP plans lack credibility and investment. `[ps=5]`
- Kendall Square Research is effectively dead. `[ps=5]`
- Uniprocessors hold the largest market share but generate the lowest profits. `[ps=4]`
- SMP scales well to 4 processors, fairly to 8, and rarely beyond 10 in commercial systems. `[ps=4]`
- Distributed operating systems will evolve from a cluster technology base. `[ps=4]`
- MPP will remain a side show to the SMP and cluster markets. `[ps=4]`
- IBM Sysplex clusters mainly appeal to IBM's largest existing mainframe customers and lack compelling overall price-performance. `[ps=4]`
- HP should show better clusters and stronger market messaging by mid-1995. `[ps=4]`
- Digital's shift toward alternate distribution and box-volume economics will likely damage high-end R&D and hurt long-term prospects. `[ps=4]`
- Aberdeen expects information demand to rise threefold to fivefold by the late 1990s, making complex DSS the next major commercial hurdle. `[ps=4]`
- Informix is betting the company on parallel RDBMS; the technology is good, but the company lacks board-room visibility. `[ps=4]`
- Oracle Parallel Server inherits VAXcluster-style limits, lacks data partitioning, and performs poorly for OLTP; a 160-processor nCube was cited as slower than a 20-processor Sequent SMP. `[ps=4]`
- The cluster version of DB2/6000 is realistically a mainstream 1996 product, not a 1994-ready one. `[ps=4]`
- Red Brick is adding SMP parallelism for DSS, while IRI Express and Prism-style utilities address multidimensional analysis and warehouse loading. `[ps=4]`
- High availability requires clusters, and OLTP benefits from spreading many short tasks across many CPUs. `[ps=4]`
- Commercial programmers should not have to parallelize code manually; the scientific parallel market suffered when automatic parallelization was weak. `[ps=4]`
- Clusters are used primarily for high availability and secondarily for resource sharing. `[ps=3]`
- Aberdeen defines MPP as a shared-nothing system with more than 100 nodes. `[ps=3]`
- HP is mediocre in SMP because it focuses on very fast RISC processors and a fewer-is-better design philosophy. `[ps=3]`
- AT&T GIS is the strongest company-wide on DSS issues and has especially deep retail and banking experience, but it is weak elsewhere. `[ps=3]`
- Oracle wants to beat IBM mainframes and protect Ellison's nCube investment, but its technology is behind the market. `[ps=3]`
- The late 1990s will be dominated by object-oriented software issues. `[ps=3]`
- Himalaya sits in the enterprise-server sweet spot, while Integrity NR/FT covers the commercial server mass market with a strong high-availability story. `[ps=3]`
- The next three years will bring an explosion of messaging-aware systems software, including Informix DSA, Sybase Navigation Server, CORBA, and DCE. `[ps=3]`
- DB2/6000 is not yet competitive with independent RDBMS products, and IBM is straining partner relationships by bidding against them. `[ps=3]`
