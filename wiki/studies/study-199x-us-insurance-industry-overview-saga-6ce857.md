# U.S. Insurance Industry Overview

> This presentation profiles the late-1990s U.S. insurance industry for Software AG's North American middleware business, combining market sizing, business drivers, industry trends, and an insurance claims architecture example. It argues that insurance offers a large but demanding opportunity for SAGA SOM because insurers face disintermediation, e-commerce, Y2K, and data-management pressures while remaining skeptical of immature middleware and new suppliers. Prepared circa 1998, the deck also proposes partner categories spanning insurance ISVs and integrators.


_Published 1998, author **Peter S. Kastner (Chief Research Officer, Aberdeen Group)**, type **market-study**._


## Top observations

- Multi-tier selling and service schemes remain standard `[ps=5]`
- Electronic commerce will proliferate across supplier-to-consumer, business-to-business, and supplier-to-intermediary channels `[ps=5]`
- Customer service is both a major cost-savings target and a differentiation tool `[ps=4]`
- State and federal regulation make market entry costly `[ps=4]`
- Mainframes remain the tradition for OLTP and batch workloads `[ps=4]`
- Agency systems are moving toward Windows NT `[ps=4]`
- Multi-tier, geographically distributed systems are the insurance norm `[ps=4]`
- Insurance demands large underwriting, actuarial, claims, and regulatory data stores, making data warehousing a natural fast-growing capability `[ps=4]`
- Large transaction volumes favor mature software `[ps=4]`
- Complex-data platforms such as ORDBMS are slow to take off in insurance `[ps=4]`
- Imaging adoption has been slow because of cost, but cheaper digital cameras and printers are improving the economics `[ps=4]`
- Example insurer has heavy mainframe investment and slow information turnaround because of batch orientation and no data warehouse `[ps=4]`
- An electronic insurance supply chain is inevitable but gradual, and software linking insurers, agencies, and reinsurers should be high value `[ps=4]`
- Insurers understand RAS, scalability, interoperability, and security, and they face difficult data-management and process-complexity problems `[ps=4]`
- SAG lacks new mainframe database managers, weakening its fit against incumbent data-management choices `[ps=4]`
- Insurers will reject the idea that SOM can be plug-and-play with legacy applications `[ps=4]`
- Insurers often restrict themselves to a small set of strategic suppliers and may exclude SAGA `[ps=4]`
- Insurers will question whether SAGA can out-middleware IBM and Microsoft `[ps=4]`
- Competition, weak actuarial bets, and product commoditization are eroding profitability `[ps=3]`
- Citibank/Travelers merger is evidence that convergence is structural, not an aberration `[ps=3]`
- Local offices need client-server systems for speed and may keep a local database `[ps=3]`
- Casualty claims should be resolved in walk-up fashion across 500 local offices, with any office able to support and update a claim `[ps=3]`
- Each office keeps a complete active claims copy while transactions are pulled to an enterprise superserver database of record, pushed back to offices, and transformed into a data warehouse `[ps=3]`
- The target workload exceeds traditional SMP and many clusters, and the platform must absorb 15%-20% annual business-volume growth `[ps=3]`
- Insurance is a major software market with strong vendor attention from IBM, HP, Sun, SAP, and Microsoft plus a large identifiable U.S. prospect base `[ps=3]`
