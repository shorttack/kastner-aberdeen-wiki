# The Long Fall: Compaq Computer, 1997-2002


## Short-horizon prescience (3-year / 5-year)

- **3-year verdict:** [DEFERRED] — SH pending
- **5-year verdict:** [DEFERRED] — SH pending

> A first-person account of Compaq's 1997-2002 decline arguing the Tandem/DEC acquisitions were flight from an already-lost PC race, not its cause. Covers the Pieper episode, the DEC integration, the Pfeiffer ouster, the Capellas stabilization, and the HP merger, ending in May 2002.


_Published 2026, author **Peter S. Kastner**, type **topic-analysis**._


## Top observations

- Compaq's 1998-99 revenue growth was DEC/Tandem arithmetic, not organic vigor; the quality of earnings deteriorated underneath the headline. `[ps=5]`
- Pfeiffer answered the wrong question ('how do we become IBM?') when the right one was 'how do we build to order before Dell buries us?' `[ps=5]`
- Tandem NonStop/Himalaya, treated as a legacy afterthought next to DEC's Alpha, was the crown jewel — a durable but narrow survivor still running mission-critical finance/telecom as HPE NonStop in 2026. `[ps=5]`
- Kastner underweighted how completely a product advantage — Compaq often had the better-engineered machine — could be rendered irrelevant by a business-model disadvantage. `[ps=5]`
- Compaq's story is acquisition used as a substitute for business-model transformation; it bought what it lacked but could not integrate it into a coherent operating entity able to defend the PC franchise. `[ps=5]`
- Compaq's Tandem/DEC acquisitions were not the disease but the flight from it — a lunge for the enterprise because the PC low ground was turning to sand under Dell. `[ps=4]`
- Roel Pieper's five-month tenure is the thesis in miniature: the man hired to build the direct channel could not survive a company constitutionally wedded to its 40,000 dealers. `[ps=4]`
- The DEC integration failed on culture: Digital's engineering-autonomy/consensus culture was the near-perfect inverse of Compaq's cost-disciplined Houston machine. `[ps=4]`
- The dealer loyalty that made Compaq great in the 1980s forced it to absorb its own forecasting-mistake losses rather than let the channel bleed — a wound Dell's near-zero inventory never carried. `[ps=4]`
- Capellas stabilized a company whose core PC economics could not be stabilized, because the model was the problem and the model had not changed. `[ps=4]`
- The forgotten inversion: Compaq paid $9.6B for the DEC assets that died (Alpha, Tru64) and $3B for the asset that lived (Tandem NonStop/Himalaya) — nobody weighted them that way at the time, Kastner included. `[ps=4]`
- Aberdeen named the central threat early: the 1997 Dell-surpasses-Compaq call (5/5), the pre-DEC services-gap diagnosis, and skepticism that an inward HP-Compaq integration would hand the field to Dell/IBM/Sun. `[ps=4]`
- Kastner attended the May 2001 Compaq analyst day in Cambridge and discussed customer communication with Compaq's analyst-relations side (Blackmore); the podium optimism was not supported by the arithmetic in the room. `[ps=4]`
- Kastner watched the retail direct-channel experiment die in Oct 2002 when athome.compaq.com redirected to hpshopping.com and build-your-own Compaq disappeared — 'quite a loss for the consumer.' `[ps=2]`
- Kastner had lunch with Michael Capellas in fall 1999; he was focused and anxious to move forward in confidence. `[ps=0]`
