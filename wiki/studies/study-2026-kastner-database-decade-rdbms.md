# The Database Decade: How Relational Databases, and the Stopwatch I Held to Them, Became the Best Work of My Aberdeen Years


## Short-horizon prescience (3-year / 5-year)

- **3-year verdict:** [DEFERRED] — SH pending
- **5-year verdict:** [DEFERRED] — SH pending

> A first-person synthesis of the RDBMS era through the lens of audited performance benchmarking — from the 1985 DebitCredit paper and the TPC through the client-server vendor wars (Oracle, Informix, Sybase, Ingres) to the x86/Linux endgame. Argues the database was the most consequential enterprise technology of Kastner's career.


_Published 2026, author **Peter S. Kastner**, type **topic-analysis**._


## Top observations

- Ingres's fate is the clearest lesson of the jungle: in enterprise software, technical superiority is necessary but nowhere near sufficient to survive; the market rewarded Oracle's salesforce over Ingres's engineering. `[ps=5]`
- Over-believed in Digital's transaction-processing future at the 1988 Blue Monday event; the archive scored that call low (mean 1.95 across 42 observations), and the author agrees it deserved to. `[ps=5]`
- At a BMC Software luncheon at the Park Lane Hotel in London, the first question from UK journalists was 'What proof do you have that the relational model will win out in the market?' — 'Gotta love UK journalists.' `[ps=5]`
- Described Sybase as 'a race car — fast, but not yet a daily driver,' technically brilliant but never quite the safe enterprise default. `[ps=5]`
- The database is not a feature of an application but the foundation of an enterprise's information architecture — the central inversion of the whole career. `[ps=4]`
- The audited benchmark was a forcing function that improved the product itself: vendors who had to publish TPC numbers an auditor would sign had to build databases that were actually fast, not just ones that demoed well. `[ps=4]`
- The database was the workload that decided which hardware won; buyers chose the machine that ran their Oracle or SQL Server fastest per dollar — hardware served the database, not the other way around. `[ps=4]`
- The great enterprise applications of the 1990s (ERP, financials, early CRM) carried no database of their own; this made database vendors the silent partners of the entire applications boom and made database performance an applications problem. `[ps=4]`
- The job was to prod enterprise IS executives past the belief that relational was fine for ad-hoc queries but too slow for real transaction processing, and convert them to database-platform-first purchasing. `[ps=4]`
- The Blue Monday forecast was wrong because the platform died within a decade, not because the measurements lied — a distinction treated as the spine of the whole career: better to be wrong about the future with an honest number than right by accident with a slogan. `[ps=4]`
- The database story and the transaction-processing story are the same story told from two ends: OLTP taught how to measure, and the RDBMS gave something worth measuring. `[ps=4]`
- 'When I was right, I was right about the direction of the river, not the fate of any particular boat' — right about technology arcs, wrong or agnostic about which vendor would ride them. `[ps=4]`
- The consistent error was assuming technical merit and honest measurement would decide the market; Oracle won not because it was the best database but because Ellison built the best sales machine behind a competent-enough database. `[ps=4]`
- Admired Ingres's engineering — rated it best-in-class — and watched it lose the market anyway; the report-card scoring could not change the sales outcome. `[ps=4]`
- The 1993 Report Card inversion — choose the database platform and let it dictate the hardware — is called 'the truest call of my career.' `[ps=4]`
- The migrated dot-com's new HP 9000/Oracle system went in smoothly and everyone was ecstatic; the following year the dot-com went bust anyway — 'such was the late 1990s Internet land grab.' `[ps=4]`
- Described Oracle in talks as the established sedan — 'car keys, next year's models' — broad and everywhere; a compliment disguised as a shrug. `[ps=4]`
- Described Ingres, painfully, as 'the slow, late vehicle, losing the race' — accurate, and by the author's own admission, a eulogy. `[ps=4]`
- IBM's own research lab invented the relational model and SQL, then IBM spent roughly fifteen years as a follower in the market it had created — the great paradox of the relational era. `[ps=4]`
- The only honest way to talk about databases was to measure them — TPS, dollars per transaction, price/performance under audited, repeatable load; everything else in the database business was marketing. `[ps=3]`
- Five structural calls landed: relational as enterprise foundation (1993); universal/object-relational servers; parallel-scalability breaking the downsizing logjam; x86 killing proprietary big iron by 2003; OLTP going open off the mainframe. `[ps=3]`
- Described Informix as 'an Aerostar van — practical, profitable, room to grow' in sales-audience talks. `[ps=3]`
- The relational database won its argument so completely that the industry stopped having it; that total victory is precisely why the database decade fades from memory, and exactly why it deserves this record. `[ps=3]`
- Called Informix practical and profitable via the 'Aerostar van' framing and could not foresee the accounting fraud that later gutted the company. `[ps=2]`
- Ended that same London afternoon at Speaker's Corner shouting about the doom of Y2K, calling the experience cathartic. `[ps=1]`
