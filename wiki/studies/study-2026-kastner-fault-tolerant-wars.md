# The Fault-Tolerant Wars, and The Last Call: NonStop, Stratus, System/88, DECtp, and Tandem's Engineered Exit


## Short-horizon prescience (3-year / 5-year)

- **3-year verdict:** [DEFERRED] — SH pending
- **5-year verdict:** [DEFERRED] — SH pending

> A first-person history of fault-tolerant computing from 1970s public-safety fail-over through the Tandem/Stratus/System-88/DECtp battles, plus 'The Last Call' account of Tandem's 1992-1997 decline and the 1994 board presentation Kastner gave. NonStop technology outlived the company.


_Published 2026, author **Peter S. Kastner**, type **topic-analysis**._


## Top observations

- ADL dispatch fail-over taught Kastner the real question wasn't whether hardware detected a fault, but whether work was designed so failure didn't become paralysis. `[ps=5]`
- Tandem's early genius was not solving OLTP as a packaged market -- it was building a fault-tolerance substrate at the level where failure first had to be controlled. `[ps=5]`
- DECtp was right that OLTP wouldn't stay confined to special-purpose machines and that benchmarks would matter, but DEC fought a platform war just as platform economics shifted beneath it. `[ps=5]`
- Sequoia should stand as an exemplar, not an exception: the FT need was real, but sales cycles, credibility burden, and channel economics were brutal for smaller FT startups. `[ps=5]`
- The winners turned recovery into a market: a buyer vocabulary, a benchmark, a price-performance claim, a channel story, a risk argument, and a credible promise that failure wouldn't sink the business. `[ps=5]`
- Tandem's decline was structural, not managerial: the market for proprietary fault-tolerant hardware was always going to be absorbed by commodity clustering and software-defined reliability. `[ps=5]`
- Perkins established: Tandem's costly hardware lagged the microprocessor shift; its OS excelled at TP but was mediocre elsewhere; its costs were assaulted by 'good enough' x86 servers. `[ps=5]`
- The early Tandem system Kastner saw at Citibank in 1977 was bare-bones: OS, checkpointing, and file system -- no database at all. `[ps=4]`
- Stratus made failure invisible to software; Tandem made failure survivable through software discipline -- the defining architectural split of the FT wars. `[ps=4]`
- The IBM System/88 relationship was a useful contradiction: IBM borrowed Stratus technology to defend accounts it also wanted to own outright with its own systems. `[ps=4]`
- Fault tolerance began as minicomputers keeping police dispatch alive and became a war among Tandem, Stratus, IBM, and DEC; the category narrowed but the promise became universal and table-stakes. `[ps=4]`
- Treybig's 1994 Compaq buyout overture shows he saw the horizon clearly; his tragedy was seeing it from inside a culture he'd built for permanence, which had become a liability. `[ps=4]`
- Whether the Compaq acquisition was a rescue or a consumption was, honestly, both -- the answer analysts rarely give because clients prefer a clean verdict. `[ps=4]`
- The technology outlasted the company: NonStop still processed most world stock transactions and credit-card authorizations years after Tandem ceased to exist independently. `[ps=4]`
- Kastner's read of the room was that the board was praying for an overlooked solution out of a big, shrinking box; he didn't have one, and concluded there wasn't one. `[ps=4]`
- Driving from Cupertino to SFO for the redeye to Boston, Kastner knew he'd heard 'the last call' -- Tandem would not be the same again, and he needed new clients. `[ps=4]`
- Kastner only 'sorta' realized in 1994 that the dot-com boom would take off in 1995 -- a partial, hedged foresight noted in hindsight. `[ps=4]`
- Roel Pieper did strip the software from Tandem's proprietary boxes, which is exactly what let Compaq and then HP keep Tandem's IP running critical workloads to this day. `[ps=4]`
- Kastner recalls the Plaza Hotel launch, July 1988, was built around Debit-Credit benchmark evidence, because benchmark discipline was the only way to escape vendor specmanship. `[ps=3]`
- Pieper's contribution was real: he made Tandem a credible acquisition target rather than a distressed one; the Microsoft alliance and NT repositioning were genuine strategic moves, not illusions. `[ps=3]`
- Kastner and Perkins walked through Tandem's 'morphing' opportunities: NonStop message-passing OS as crown jewel, object-oriented database/transaction/office/messaging as late-1990s growth candidates. `[ps=2]`
- Kastner recalls first seeing an early Tandem NonStop system in 1977 at Citibank while working on the SWIFT funds-transfer design. `[ps=0]`
- Kastner led the DECtp marketing strategy: costly and tedious to execute, spending months and hundreds of thousands of dollars proving IBM mainframe OLTP inferiority using IBM's own systems engineers. `[ps=0]`
- Jimmy Treybig's office called Kastner around Labor Day 1994 and asked him to address the Tandem board on September 27, 1994; he agreed. `[ps=0]`
- Kastner's prepared topic, 'Surfing the Parallel Architectures,' ran 28 slides plus 13 backup slides; he expected, rightly, an unrehearsed grilling by the NYSE-listed board. `[ps=0]`
