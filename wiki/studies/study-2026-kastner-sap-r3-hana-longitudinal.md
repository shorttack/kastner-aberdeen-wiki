# SAP R/3 to S/4HANA and the Platforms Beneath It: An Aberdeen Longitudinal, 1991-2026


## Short-horizon prescience (3-year / 5-year)

- **3-year verdict:** [DEFERRED] — SH pending
- **5-year verdict:** [DEFERRED] — SH pending

> A first-person synthesis of the SAP R/3 platform war as the Aberdeen archive recorded it — HP/Oracle/Unix high end, Wintel/SQL Server mid-range, Dell/Oracle/Linux endgame — threaded through the Oracle-SAP coopetition and closing with HANA, the move Kastner advised against in 2001.


_Published 2026, author **Peter S. Kastner**, type **topic-analysis**._


## Top observations

- Certifiable top-of-tier engineering is necessary but not sufficient for market survival: Sequent had the certified SD benchmark and the SAP NT Competence Center and still got absorbed by IBM — ecosystem gravity beat point-technical superiority. `[ps=5]`
- Across the whole arc, R/3's failure mode was never the box or the database — it was organizations that never honestly assessed their desire for change before throwing the 10,000 switches. `[ps=5]`
- The systems-integrator layer (Andersen's 300 SAP/Siebel technicians as the tell) became a structural power center in the R/3 economy that the vendor-and-platform framing under-modeled at the time. `[ps=5]`
- SAP's own self-description as a 'server Switzerland, neutral to all comers' could hold for hardware but never for the database, because its database vendor was simultaneously its applications rival — neutrality had a structural limit. `[ps=5]`
- Kastner read SAP's database dependency on Oracle as a convenience to optimize; SAP read the same dependency as a strategic vulnerability to eliminate — he underweighted how badly SAP wanted independence. `[ps=5]`
- Oracle/SAP was the strangest coopetition in the industry: Oracle's database was R/3's safest, most-proven reference port while Oracle's applications division was SAP's most dangerous rival. `[ps=4]`
- The arc closes where the archive itself did not expect: SAP walked away from every third-party database to build HANA — the one move Kastner explicitly advised against in 2001. `[ps=4]`
- Every 'whose server?' R/3 decision from 1993-2004 was really a bet on a RISC/CISC architecture and an RDBMS at once; the platform question was a proxy war for the database and the OS. `[ps=4]`
- Coopetition of this kind is inherently unstable and resolves toward vertical integration; SAP could not indefinitely depend on a database vendor that also sold competing applications, so HANA was the inevitable resolution. `[ps=4]`
- One fact — HP as the shared RISC/Unix reference port for both SAP and Oracle — decided more R/3 deals in the mid-1990s than any single benchmark; ecosystem-reference status outweighed technical comparison. `[ps=4]`
- 2026 rebuttal: Kastner knew in-memory DBs ran 10-100x faster than disk-bound RDBMS even in 2001, but at ~$1/MB ($16,000 for 16GB on a Compaq 4-way) there wasn't enough affordable memory to run Fortune 1000 workloads in-memory yet. `[ps=4]`
- For a dozen years the real question was never whether to buy R/3 but what to run it on and whose database sat underneath — the platform fight, not the product decision, is the story. `[ps=3]`
- Right about the arc even when recommending a box history would obsolete: Aberdeen called R/3's dominance in 1993 and never walked it back, and correctly named HP as the platform that would win the 1990s Unix tier. `[ps=3]`
- The archive correctly certified Sequent as the top of tier 3 but didn't reckon with how quickly SMP engineering credibility could evaporate into an $810M IBM acquisition before achieving enterprise dominance. `[ps=3]`
- Author testimony (Denis George, HP, not in archive): HP laid off 4,000 COBOL programmers to cut costs, then began throwing R/3's 10,000 configuration switches, only to discover the process knowledge it needed lived in the people it had just laid off. `[ps=3]`
- Denis George's own verdict on HP's R/3 install: 'SAP R3 was only for the foolhardy and well-funded' — devastating because it came from the reference-platform vendor itself, the company whose iron was the R/3 gold standard. `[ps=3]`
- The Plattner pitch worked because a sales force in 35% annual churn cannot defend its accounts; Kastner reads SAP's rise as owing as much to Oracle's internal weakness as to R/3's own strengths. `[ps=3]`
- Even as SAP plotted database independence, the 2004 platform winner (Dell/Xeon/Linux) ran on Oracle 9i RAC — proof the two companies could not fully quit each other even at the moment Unix itself was being killed off. `[ps=3]`
- Author testimony (not in archive): Kastner and John Logan met Hasso Plattner in Boston circa 1999, near the end of the analyst day queue; Plattner opened curtly, 'Why should I waste my time talking to analysts?' `[ps=0]`
- Author testimony (not in archive): Kastner pitched Plattner that Aberdeen had 'actionable intelligence and a plan for SAP to rapidly gain market share against Oracle,' then revealed Oracle's employee turnover had reached 35% — Plattner's demeanor turned congenial and the meeting ran over time. `[ps=0]`
- Author testimony: Kastner and John Logan worked the Plattner conversation like a rehearsed rod-and-reel — Logan (a Harvard MBA) took the cast the moment the Oracle turnover number landed, without prior coordination. `[ps=0]`
- HANA has no first-party study in the archive; the in-memory pivot is visible only as retrospective outcome observations embedded inside older studies, leaving this arc closed on secondary rather than primary evidence. `[ps=0]`
- The 2001 Compaq Tru64/Oracle9i recommendation — that SAP should embrace and not resist the Compaq/Oracle partnership — sat exactly backwards to what SAP actually did; scored low, the most instructive miss in the archive.
- The real mistake was not the in-memory cost math itself but failing to anticipate the competitive/frictional advantage SAP would gain by replacing Oracle's cost with margin flowing to SAP's own HANA — SAP was not usually early to innovate, but it was with HANA.
