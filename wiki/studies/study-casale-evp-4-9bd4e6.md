# Are Hypergrowth P&Ls Real? — Or Are You Seeing More Than You Paid For

> Executive Viewpoint #4 analyzes the P&L implications of hypergrowth by introducing the Revenue Pull versus Cost Push framework and demonstrating with detailed quarterly financial tables for the fictional composite firm FTI. Casale shows how cost-push operations — where expenses run ahead of sales — can mask earnings quality deterioration even when EPS targets are met, because front-loaded costs require incremental revenue just to maintain margin, and the resulting pressure incentivizes GAAP-violating practices. The analysis previews balance sheet effects as the next analytical layer.

## Top observations

- Factory management planned push on February 29 1982 (non-leap year); shipments went out during wee hours of February 30; shipping dock date stamp stuck on February 28. `[ps=5]`
- Stage 1 (legitimate): Sales promotions, discounting, compensation adders. Stage 2 (legitimate): Borrowing sales from future periods via backlog. Stage 3 (legitimate): New product introductions, customer incentives, extended payment terms. Stage 4 (GAAP-violating): Cover the clock at quarter end. Stage 5: Book shipments where paperwork is questionable. Stage 6: Take contingency sales into revenue.  `[ps=5]`
- Auditors (CPA firm) are not paid to tell management how to smooth earnings; quarterly review less stringent than annual audit; stopped-clock shipment timing would require exhaustive investigation to detect. `[ps=5]`
- Revenue Pull: manage business for smooth order/shipment flow even at cost of market share; emphasis on cost control. Cost Push: ramp product development, marketing, and capacity ahead of hard orders; emphasis on market share. Most fast-track firms operate in Cost Push mode. `[ps=4]`
- Nearly $20 million of incremental sales required just to cover the incremental expenses running ahead of plan. `[ps=4]`
- Quarterly financial statements are technically 'interim statements': neither audited nor carry same weight as annual statements. Many annual expenses (benefits, annual report costs) occur in peaks but are spread over quarters per matching principle. `[ps=4]`
- After a banner year, investors and management mutually reinforce each other; explicit urgings to make FY83 even better; the cycle resets at a higher baseline of expectation. `[ps=4]`
- $2 million of advertising expenses lost in rudimentary control system; had been pushed from Q1 into Q2. Impact: 6 cents EPS at $110M revenue — equivalent to Q1 earnings. `[ps=3]`
