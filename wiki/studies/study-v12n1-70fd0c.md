# R K E T V I E W P O I N T

> This study analyzes R K E T V I E W P O I N T using Aberdeen Group research material and prepared extraction artifacts.


_Published 1999, author **Aberdeen Group**, type **market-study**._


## Top observations

- — commonly referred to as Maintenance, Repair, and Operating (MRO) supplies — typically account for 30% to 35% of a manufacturer’s total expenditures and can also account for more than half the dollars spent by service and municipal organizations. `[ps=5]`
- For example, a $5-million reduction in procurement costs increases profits by a corresponding amount. `[ps=5]`
- Thus, even a company with a healthy 10% profit margin would need to increase sales by $50 million to attain a similar boost in profits. `[ps=5]`
- All told, U.S. businesses spend $1.4 trillion on nonproduction goods and services each year, according to estimates from _Purchasing Magazine._ Yet, until recently, few organizations have had an effective strategy for managing and controlling MRO expenditures. `[ps=5]`
- Rerouting maverick purchases onto existing corporate agreements enables organizations to take advantage of contract prices and volume discounts previously negotiated with approved suppliers (Figure 1). `[ps=5]`
- User organizations are currently leveraging Internet Procurement automation as a mechanism to support the purchase-to-order cycle for MRO goods and services (Figure 3). `[ps=5]`
- It also eases the management burden for suppliers, allowing them to develop and maintain a single Web-based catalog for all customers (Figure 4). `[ps=5]`
- Fewer than 20% of the sites surveyed reported that their installations supported real-time transaction processing with trading partner systems. `[ps=5]`
- Buying organizations surveyed by Aberdeen reported that it cost $30, on average, to process an order through an automated procurement system, compared with $107-per-requisition for orders processed manually. `[ps=5]`
- Maverick buys account for as much as 40% of all MRO spending at some organizations, limiting an organization’s ability to effectively track MRO purchases; diminishing opportunities to take advantage of prenegotiated prices and volume discounts; and, reducing lev- `[ps=4]`
- If 40% of MRO spending is off-contract, total maverick spending = $100 million/yr. `[ps=4]`
- If half of maverick spending were brought under corporate purchasing agreements — each with modest pricing discounts of 10% off the list price — total savings = $5 million/yr., or a 5% increase in profits. `[ps=4]`
- However, all sites surveyed had plans to eventually extend Internet Procurement to the entire enterprise (Figure 2). `[ps=4]`
- Most early adopters expected to activate fewer than 10% of their existing MRO suppliers on the system, indicating that buying organizations are conducting significant supplier rationalization exercises prior to implementing Internet Procurement automation. `[ps=4]`
- This conclusion is supported by user organization estimates that the level of effort required to support system interfaces — both internally and externally — comprises nearly one-third of all maintenance activities for procurement automation (Figure 5). `[ps=4]`
- In fact, early adopters surveyed reported annual MRO expenditures of $200 million or more, indicating a return on investment (ROI) exceeding 300% for reductions in maverick spending alone. `[ps=3]`
- The average selling price for Internet Procurement applications installed at the user sites surveyed was $1.4 million, including license fees, implementation services fees, and initial maintenance fees. `[ps=2]`
