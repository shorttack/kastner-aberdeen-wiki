# Chapter 9: Expert Witness — Technology on Trial (1992–2015)

> Peter Kastner recounts a parallel career as a technology expert witness spanning 1992–2015, alongside his Aberdeen Group research practice. Key engagements include a major data-warehousing antitrust dispute involving Safeway and a Teradata machine, software project failure forensics, and a Florida statewide welfare-system bid protest in which Kastner’s IBM performance analysis was later validated by the project’s real-world failure. The chapter distills methodological lessons: governance failure almost always precedes technical failure, market-research frameworks map directly onto litigation analysis, and adversarial expert scrutiny instills a permanent standard of rigor.


_Published 2026, author **Peter S. Kastner**, type **memoir**._


## Top observations

- Received unsolicited call from unknown law firm seeking expert on enterprise database technology; said yes, opening a parallel career spanning 20+ years.
- Complex commercial litigation involving enterprise database technology; required expertise in relational databases at architectural and market level.
- Expert witness practice ran alongside Aberdeen research practice for more than two decades (1992–2015).
- Consulting expert: confidential, candid, not disclosed. Testifying expert: formal opinions, depositions, cross-examination — every opinion must be bulletproof.
- Three routes: former senior engineers, former CIOs, and technology analysts with research credentials. Analyst credential proved advantageous.
- Aberdeen’s published research and TPC work gave Kastner credentials courts found credible for technology expert testimony.
- As analyst (not engineer), Kastner could speak to what industry knew, what market expected, and what competent technology practice looked like — the standards by which decisions are judged.
- Cases ranged from database licensing disputes to software project failures to antitrust claims in the storage market.
- Most significant litigation engagement of Kastner’s expert witness career: major antitrust and breach-of-contract dispute.
- Centered on data warehousing technology: what had been promised, what had been delivered, whether competitive market analysis informing the deal was sound, and damages.
- Second-seat to novice expert Hugh Bishop in the Safeway engagement.
- By mid-1990s, data warehouses had become strategic infrastructure for large retailers, consolidating POS, supplier, and inventory data for analytical decision-making.
- Better analytical capability translated directly into better purchasing negotiations, more effective promotions, and reduced inventory costs — all affecting margins in a thin-margin business.
- Safeway’s Teradata data warehouse machine was effectively destroyed when the fire extinguishing system exploded into the closed computer room.
- Role was damages expert: built analytical model to answer what market value of contracted technology was, how it differed from delivery, and economic consequence of that gap.
- Drew on Aberdeen’s competitive landscape analysis, TCO models, and performance benchmarking methodology; research and litigation used the same analytical tools in different contexts.
- Ran over three years, involved dozens of depositions, produced hundreds of pages of expert reports.
- Settlement terms confidential; technical and economic analysis admitted into evidence; established market value of enterprise data warehousing in terms courts could understand.
- Case established market value of enterprise data warehousing capabilities in terms courts could understand and apply — described as the fundamental contribution of expert testimony.
- When a data warehouse project failed or underperformed, damages were calculable but only with expert analysis quantifying properly functioning vs. failed system performance.
- Initial contracts described systems at a level of abstraction both parties could agree to but left implementation details undefined; client and vendor diverged on what the system should do.
- Business environments change; ERP implementations starting 1999 encountered Y2K remediation priorities in 1998; change orders introduced adversarial dynamics.
- New system always had to connect to existing systems never adequately described in the contract; integration scope was the site of the most damaging disputes.
- User acceptance testing frequently rushed to meet contractual go-live dates; defects discovered in production were far more expensive to remediate.
- Roughly 30% succeed as defined; 50% succeed partially; 20% fail completely. Rates have improved modestly with agile, cloud, SaaS but persist over 30 years.
