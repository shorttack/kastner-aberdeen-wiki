# Chapter 9: Expert Witness — Technology on Trial (1992–2015)

> Peter Kastner recounts a parallel career as a technology expert witness spanning 1992–2015, alongside his Aberdeen Group research practice. Key engagements include a major data-warehousing antitrust dispute involving Safeway and a Teradata machine, software project failure forensics, and a Florida statewide welfare-system bid protest in which Kastner’s IBM performance analysis was later validated by the project’s real-world failure. The chapter distills methodological lessons: governance failure almost always precedes technical failure, market-research frameworks map directly onto litigation analysis, and adversarial expert scrutiny instills a permanent standard of rigor.


_Published 2026, author **Peter S. Kastner**, type **memoir**._


## Top observations

- By mid-1990s, data warehouses had become strategic infrastructure for large retailers, consolidating POS, supplier, and inventory data for analytical decision-making. `[ps=5]`
- Better analytical capability translated directly into better purchasing negotiations, more effective promotions, and reduced inventory costs — all affecting margins in a thin-margin business. `[ps=5]`
- Initial contracts described systems at a level of abstraction both parties could agree to but left implementation details undefined; client and vendor diverged on what the system should do. `[ps=5]`
- New system always had to connect to existing systems never adequately described in the contract; integration scope was the site of the most damaging disputes. `[ps=5]`
- User acceptance testing frequently rushed to meet contractual go-live dates; defects discovered in production were far more expensive to remediate. `[ps=5]`
- Roughly 30% succeed as defined; 50% succeed partially; 20% fail completely. Rates have improved modestly with agile, cloud, SaaS but persist over 30 years. `[ps=5]`
- In software project disputes, the technical problems that ended up in court were almost never the first sign of trouble; before code was bad, governance was bad; before system failed, communication had failed. `[ps=5]`
- Data formats, transaction volumes, error handling requirements, and performance expectations of integrations with existing systems were discovered during implementation, not defined upfront. `[ps=5]`
- Governance failures included executive pressure that overrode technical judgment at critical decision points — a root cause found in software project failures. `[ps=5]`
- Complex commercial litigation involving enterprise database technology; required expertise in relational databases at architectural and market level. `[ps=4]`
- As analyst (not engineer), Kastner could speak to what industry knew, what market expected, and what competent technology practice looked like — the standards by which decisions are judged. `[ps=4]`
- Business environments change; ERP implementations starting 1999 encountered Y2K remediation priorities in 1998; change orders introduced adversarial dynamics. `[ps=4]`
- In 20 years of expert witness work on software project failures, encountered genuine technical incompetence perhaps 5 times; encountered governance and organizational failures in every single case. `[ps=4]`
- Three questions: (1) What did the contract actually require? (2) What was actually delivered? (3) What were the consequences? `[ps=4]`
- Include: cost of work paid for but not received; cost of remediation; operational losses during underperformance period; competitive disadvantage from market position loss. `[ps=4]`
- Courts see markets through transactions that failed; most market participants experience markets through transactions that succeeded. Expert witness work provides the failure-side perspective. `[ps=4]`
- Enterprise technology contracts are frequently written in language both parties understand differently; ambiguity is sometimes how deals get done; cost paid at implementation or in court. `[ps=4]`
- An ERP implementation starting in 1999 encountered Y2K remediation priority in late 1998; business environment changes faster than contracts can accommodate. `[ps=4]`
- A healthcare system under development in 2001 faced a different regulatory environment by 2003 — illustrating how requirements change faster than contracts can accommodate. `[ps=4]`
- Change order negotiations consumed enormous energy and introduced adversarial dynamics into what should have been a collaborative client-vendor relationship. `[ps=4]`
- Client and vendor operated from different mental models of the same contract document; neither was necessarily acting in bad faith. `[ps=4]`
- Three routes: former senior engineers, former CIOs, and technology analysts with research credentials. Analyst credential proved advantageous. `[ps=3]`
- When a data warehouse project failed or underperformed, damages were calculable but only with expert analysis quantifying properly functioning vs. failed system performance. `[ps=3]`
- Research conclusions presented with confidence levels; legal opinions presented with certainty. The underlying analysis is identical. `[ps=3]`
- Aberdeen’s analytical frameworks — market definition, competitive comparison, TCO modeling, performance benchmarking — were the same frameworks courts needed. Translation was only methodological. `[ps=2]`
