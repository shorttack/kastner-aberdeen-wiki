# Introduction: The Physics of the Machine

> Peter Kastner opens his fifty-year memoir by tracing his organizing conviction—that machines should handle paper work so people can handle everything else—to childhood summers sorting receipts in a Cape Cod restaurant and observing the labor-intensive nature of American commerce. The introduction frames the book as neither a standard industry history nor a conventional business autobiography, but as an insider's account of how information technology collides with institutions, economics, and human nature across successive eras. Kastner previews five analytical frameworks developed during the Aberdeen years, the book's chronological-but-modular structure, and its deliberate shift from third-person to first-person narration.


_Published 2026, author **Peter S. Kastner**, type **memoir**._


## Top observations

- Kastner worked every summer from age twelve in his family's Cape Cod restaurant, which served more than 1,000 diners a night.
- Job was to reconcile tens of thousands of paper slips: every drink, appetizer, bottle of wine traced to a check, matched to a cashier deposit, to the penny.
- The Federal Reserve employed half a million people sorting and re-touching checks as they moved through the American banking system.
- Commerce was extraordinarily labor intensive; making an airline reservation required a half-hour phone call and a trip to a travel agent to collect a physical ticket.
- Before leaving for college, Kastner decided that machines should handle the paper and people should handle everything else.
- Information technology is never just about technology—it is about the collision of systems with institutions, economics, and human nature.
- Every information system is really an argument with reality.
- Kastner's understanding of computing's social role developed gradually at MIT.
- One of Kastner's early computing workplaces was a converted funeral home in Arlington, MA.
- Kastner participated in a securities migration project at Chase Manhattan Bank.
- Kastner worked inside the Stratus fault-tolerant lab in Marlborough, MA.
- Kastner worked through DEC's enterprise war rooms as part of his career.
- Kastner spent eighteen years co-founding and building Aberdeen Group.
- Aberdeen grew to 150 analysts under Kastner's leadership.
- Junior programmer to chief research officer of Aberdeen Group over fifty years.
- Batch computing, timesharing, minicomputers, fault-tolerant OLTP, client-server revolution, open systems wars, PCs and internet era, rise of research industry.
- Apollo veterans taught Kastner that sloppy code is a moral failing.
- A Y2K fix was made in 1969, thirty years before it became a global panic.
- A benchmark was run in secret using two IBM mainframes to ambush the enterprise computing industry.
- Aberdeen's cooperative revenue model worked; venture capital later changed the incentives and ultimately damaged it.
- The company that acquired Aberdeen paid a premium for a culture it could not preserve.
- A customs inspector in Rio unspooled magnetic tape onto an airport floor—cited as one of the more simply human stories in the memoir.
- A trade-show handoff produced one of the more memorable T-shirts in Route 128 history.
- Chapters follow a broadly chronological arc but can be entered independently; reader can enter at Stratus, DEC benchmark, or Aberdeen founding without losing the thread.
- Each era placed Kastner inside a different kind of machine—literal (mainframe, minicomputer, fault-tolerant system, benchmark lab) or organizational (bank, consulting firm, vendor, research company).
