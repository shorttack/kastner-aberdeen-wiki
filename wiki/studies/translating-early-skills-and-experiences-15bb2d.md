# Translating Early Skills and Experiences into Marketing and Industry Analysis

> Peter S. Kastner traces his professional arc from Machine Architect (1969–1979) through Market Planner (1979–1988) to Industry Strategist at Aberdeen Group (1988–2007), arguing that each phase translated technical 'scars' into progressively higher-level analytical tools. The memoir identifies the forensic foundation, capitalist's perspective, and 'Simple Physics' mindset as the three compounding assets that enabled cross-industry prediction at scale.

## Key observations (top 25)

- **1979** — Career arc characterization: Trajectory 1969-2007 described as continuous evolution from 'Machine Architect' to 'Industry Strategist'; technical scars of mainframe era became predictive tools of analyst years
- **1969** — Phase 1 primary skill: Micro-Efficiency: Machine time at $360/hour; far more valuable than human labor; every bit and cycle had to be justified; drove mastery of I/O optimization and dump reading
- **1969** — Phase 1 primary skill: Forensic Systems Auditing: Ability to identify system 'thrashing' or 'Unmitigated Software Mess' nine months before failure; honed at ADL and Chase Manhattan
- **1972** — Phase 1 secondary skill: Architectural Diplomacy: Designing 'bicycle-powered' systems in Rio and rural 5-baud networks in Wisconsin respecting physical and economic constraints
- **1979** — Phase 2 transition: Market Planning: At Prime Computer, technical mastery translated into Market Planning and strategic 'Go/No-Go' Logic; shift from fixing one engine to planning entire factory
- **1975** — Phase 2 enabling asset: Capitalist's Perspective: Years of trading Wang stock options and riding market cycles informed transition from technical work to market planning
- **1977** — Phase 2 asset: Deal Structure knowledge: ADL fixed-price turnkey failures became primary asset; understanding liability of unrecognized accruals and OEM contract 'billback' traps enabled creation of flexible software pricing models
- **1980** — Strategic move: Distributed minicomputers: Intuition of Moore's Law enabled move toward distributed departmental minicomputers like Prime 750 just as mainframe's price/performance monopoly was collapsing
- **1988** — Phase 3 primary skill: Economic and Technical Synthesis: 'Simple Physics' mindset used to judge entire industry; engineering baseline from 1969 provided clear view of which architectures would genuinely scale vs 'making it up as they go'
- **1990** — Phase 3 capability: Detecting corporate USMs: Ability to detect 'Unmitigated Software Messes' at corporate level; analyzing company health through technical 'Order and Harmony' not just spreadsheets
- **1976** — Reference: $12 million USM litigation: $12 million litigation at USM/Emhart referenced as formative example of forensic auditing applied at Aberdeen Group level
- **1970** — Reference: 'Watergate' atmosphere: Houghton Mifflin environment referenced as formative example of reading corporate health signals
- **1995** — Phase 3 advantage: Whole Industry Perspective: Knowing more than 'locals' due to breadth of cross-industry auditing; COBOL and Assembler background turned into predictive tool for global technology market
- **1982** — Learning: Flexible software pricing models: Learning from Sandy Kurzig at ASK; more flexible, profitable software pricing models derived from understanding OEM contract risks
- **1980** — Technology timing: Moore's Law intuition: Technical ability to intuit Moore's Law as tool for strategic technology timing decisions; informed distributed computing move
- **1969** — Technical foundation: COBOL and Assembler: Mastery of COBOL and Assembler; I/O optimization; dump reading in raw hexadecimal; foundation that later became industry prediction tool
