# Chapter 1: Waiting for Automation (1960-1969)

> Peter S. Kastner recounts his formative years growing up on Cape Cod running the bookkeeping department of his family's large restaurant, where the labor-intensive paper reconciliation problem planted the seed for a lifelong drive to automate commerce. He traces his path from Cornell University's Hotel School — where a failed punch-card tabulation experiment and an IBM 360/65 operations job deepened his technical grounding — through a year at MIT's computing center, where he encountered timesharing, early AI, and the first DEC minicomputers. The chapter closes with his contributions to Robert Chase's hotel-management simulation software at Cornell and his departure for Philip Hankins Inc. in Boston, still in pursuit of the paper problem that had never been solved.

## Key observations (top 50)

- **1947** — Kastner birth year and hometown: Born 1947; grew up in Chatham, on Cape Cod, Massachusetts.
- **1955** — Restaurant scale at peak: Six dining rooms (575 seats), cocktail lounges (200), opera house (300); June-Sept season; over 1,000 diners per night.
- **1955** — Restaurant rating: Five-star destination.
- **1960** — Paper receipts generated per evening: Approximately 10,000 pieces of paper per evening (six slips per table for 1,000+ diners), all requiring next-day reconciliation.
- **1960** — Business control rationale for paper receipts: Servers are independent business people who can collect cash and merchandise; paper trails deterred theft.
- **1960** — Age of first bookkeeping responsibility: Age twelve; described as 'Basic Business Controls 101, delivered at age twelve.'
- **1962** — Promotion to bookkeeping department manager: By his third summer at the restaurant, Kastner was promoted to manager of the bookkeeping department.
- **1962** — Tools available for bookkeeping: Adding machines, pencils, and lined accounting workbooks — no path to making the job faster or smarter.
- **1960** — Federal Reserve nightly check-sorting workforce: Nearly half a million people employed every night to sort checks through the financial system circa 1960.
- **1960** — Check-handling labor intensity: Every single check was touched by human hands at minimum half a dozen times before being returned to account holder.
- **1960** — Travel agent paper workflow: Making an airline reservation required a half-hour telephone call and a trip to the travel agent to collect a physical ticket.
- **1960** — Commerce labor intensity in 1960: Commerce was extraordinarily labor intensive, and almost nobody in 1960 thought there was anything unusual about that.
- **1965** — Cornell enrollment intention: Enrolled at Cornell's School of Hotel Administration in 1965 expecting to find automation answers there.
- **1965** — Concert lineup at Cornell arrival: First Friday concert: The Beach Boys; second: The Rolling Stones; fourth: Bob Dylan.
- **1965** — Hotel School model hotel automation level: Front desk ran on an NCR paper-tape cash register; cash procedures nearly identical to Cape Cod restaurant. No plans to change.
- **1965** — Hotel Ezra Cornell punch-card automation attempt: Upper-class accounting team used donated IBM punch-card tabulating equipment to automate Hotel Ezra Cornell (HEC) weekend books.
- **1965** — Punch-card tabulator programming mechanism: Programmed by physical wiring on a plugboard — patching cables like an old telephone switchboard — not software.
- **1965** — Punch-card tabulator conditional logic limitation: Could sort, count, and add 80-column card data but could not perform conditional logic (if-then category assignment) with sufficient flexibility via plugboard wiring.
- **1966** — HEC automation project outcome: 'Our eyes were bigger than what the technology could deliver.' Team conceded defeat; fell back on paper receipts and human bookkeepers.
- **1966** — Decision to take a year off and join MIT: Took a year off from Cornell; took a job at MIT as a full-time computer operator to learn computing at the foundation level.
- **1966** — MIT computing center IBM 1401 role: Reader-printer workhorse: ingested punch-card batches, wrote to magnetic tape at 800 bpi, printed mainframe output. 'The loading dock.'
- **1966** — MIT IBM 7094 dual-mode operation: Daytime: timesharing for dozens of simultaneous academic users. Off-peak: batch mode processing jobs queued on tape.
- **1966** — IBM 7094 timesharing historical significance: Techniques developed at MIT on the 7094 fed directly into the Multics operating system.
- **1966** — MIT IBM 360/65 role and accessories: Dedicated development machine with attached graphics workstation and trackball — 'an exotic luxury in 1966.'
- **1966** — Longevity of climate code on IBM 360/65: Base FORTRAN airflow-analysis code written on the MIT 360/65 was still embedded in climate models as late as the 2020s.
- **1966** — Batch processing economic model of 1960s computing: Three-machine pipeline (cards in, tape to mainframe, results back) defined the economics of computing for the next decade.
- **1966** — Computer operations as factory work: Noisy rooms, constant pressure to keep jobs moving, modest pay — 'factory work in a white coat.'
- **1966** — Self-teaching FORTRAN at MIT: Taught himself FORTRAN on the company's idle overnight CPU cycles — 'on the company dime.'
- **1966** — Self-teaching IBM assembler at MIT: Taught himself IBM assembler during quiet overnight hours on the operator's own accounts.
- **1966** — Spacewar! on MIT mainframe: Spacewar! video game appeared permanently installed on the 360/65 graphics workstation; Kastner could play it on a '$5 million mainframe' during overnight shifts.
- **1966** — Classified simulation work at MIT: Helped a cryptic researcher run classified simulations. Researcher said only: 'Things that go boom.'
- **1966** — Project MAC research focus: Developing foundations of timesharing and multiuser computing that would eventually produce the Multics operating system.
- **1966** — AI debate culture at MIT: Researchers argued about artificial intelligence 'with a fervor that felt more like religion than engineering.'
- **1966** — LISP use in AI at MIT: LISP used to manipulate symbolic representations of knowledge for AI research circa 1966.
- **1966** — ELIZA effect on users: ELIZA had text-based conversations that 'unnerved people who didn't know it had no inner life whatsoever.'
- **1966** — Kastner's early assessment of AI: From the operator's console, AI wasn't magic — it was just more code competing for time on the same machines that ran payroll and climate models.
- **1966** — DEC PDP/8 presence in Cambridge: Early DEC PDP/8 minicomputers were appearing in labs around Cambridge in 1966-1967.
- **1966** — Significance of early minicomputer appearance: The glass-house era — when computing meant a centralized room behind locked doors — was beginning, almost invisibly, to crack.
- **1967** — Return to Cornell with new credentials: Returned to Cornell in 1967 with dual credential: computer operations and programming experience from MIT.
- **1967** — Sole university programming job available: Only one programming job available; required PL/1 experience. Nobody had it in 1967; job went elsewhere.
- **1967** — PL/1 scarcity of experienced programmers: Asking for experienced PL/1 programmers in 1967 was 'roughly equivalent to asking for experienced ChatGPT programmers in 2020.'
- **1964** — PL/1 design intent: Designed in mid-1960s to unify FORTRAN's mathematical capability with COBOL's structured data handling — powerful but extremely new in 1967.
- **1967** — Cornell IBM 360/65 operator job and pay: Operated Cornell's brand-new IBM 360/65; paid $2.00/hour (federal minimum wage) plus unlimited computer time at no charge.
- **1967** — Dave Pulleyn assigns Kastner to Sysgen Day: Cornell operations manager Dave Pulleyn relieved to have experienced 360/65 hand; immediately assigned Kastner to Sysgen Day.
- **1967** — IBM Sysgen process description: Systems generation: computer creates its own operating software from source code and config. Loaded hundreds of punch-card decks in correct sequence; machine compiled itself.
- **1967** — IBM OS source code licensing practice in 1960s: IBM provided source code for its operating systems to customers in this era — a practice it abandoned in the 1980s as software became a separate business.
- **1967** — Cornell Sysgen target OS: Modified IBM OS/360 enhanced with HASP (Houston Automated Spooling Processor) to manage job queue.
- **1967** — Cornell Sysgen Day execution: Saturday, September 23, 1967: Arlene Larsen wheeled in punch cards and a 2311 disk pack (13 MB — 'enormous storage by the standards of the day'). Sysgen succeeded.
- **1967** — IBM 2311 disk pack capacity: 13 megabytes — described as 'an enormous amount of storage by the standards of the day.'
- **1967** — Cornell's original plan: IBM 360/67: Cornell originally planned to install an IBM 360/67 timesharing-capable variant. IBM couldn't deliver it.
