# Chapter 3: Eight Years in the Physics of Consulting (1972–1979)

> Peter Kastner recounts his eight years (1972–1979) at Arthur D. Little (ADL) in Cambridge, where he shifted from machine-level programming at Philip Hankins Inc. to broad-based consulting across banking, healthcare, manufacturing, utilities, and public safety. Through engagements at Chase Manhattan Bank, a Rio de Janeiro hospital, Continental Forest Industries, Wisconsin Electric, Hampton VA Police, Houghton Mifflin, and United Shoe Machinery, he developed a systems-failure taxonomy centered on organizational, political, and financial misalignment rather than purely technical failure. The chapter closes with Kastner’s capitalist awakening: watching the minicomputer revolution invert the economics of computing, he left ADL for Prime Computer, trading a coding pad for a market-shaping role.

## Key observations (top 50)

- **1972** — Career transition from PHI to ADL: Kastner joined ADL Systems in Cambridge after Wang Laboratories announced plans to move PHI to Tewksbury, triggering PHI diaspora.
- **1972** — ADL Systems hiring profile: PHI alumni arrived as 'small, seasoned rescue squad, hired precisely because we knew how systems actually worked rather than how they were supposed to work.'
- **1972** — PHI culture: machine-level focus: At PHI, staff lived inside the machine—core dumps, disk-arm rotations, microsecond counts. Machine was the universe with rules.
- **1972** — Systems failure locus: Most systems don’t fail in the machine; they fail in the boardroom, the warehouse, or the customs shed at the airport.
- **1972** — Chase Manhattan ADL assignment scope: Migrate Chase Manhattan Bank securities processing (~$20B in assets) from aging Univac hardware to IBM without losing a single share or penny.
- **1972** — Chase Manhattan securities assets under management: Approximately $20 billion in assets in securities processing operation.
- **1972** — Univac hardware obsolescence at Chase: Equipment described as 'archeologically obsolete'; spare parts legendarily dumped at sea; impossible to source replacement hardware at any price.
- **1973** — COBOL migration code volume: Two Cosmos experts delivered 25 million lines of bug-free COBOL code in one year.
- **1972** — 96-column vs. 80-column card format incompatibility: Univac 96-column cards vs. IBM 80-column: every Chase field designed for 96 columns had to be exploded into multiple 80-column IBM cards, rebuilding every field boundary.
- **1972** — David Rockefeller dividend field overflow: Rockefeller’s holdings were so large that the standard dividend field could not contain the numbers after column-count reduction; team spent hours hunting for one more column.
- **1972** — Parallel run human audit engine: Chase assembled a small army of temp workers to reprocess days of transactions by hand to verify both Univac and IBM systems produced identical answers.
- **1972** — Origins of automation: Automation is usually born not from a love of elegance but from sheer exhaustion with manual labor.
- **1972** — Chase migration architecture longevity: Architecture built for Chase ran for decades, surviving corporate mergers and hardware generations; DNA traces persist in modern financial plumbing.
- **1975** — Kastner status at ADL by 1975: By 1975 Kastner had become a ‘vetted asset’; clients named in proposals began requesting him personally.
- **1975** — Federal University Hospital Rio project mandate: Turnkey hospital system to serve an urban poverty area in Rio de Janeiro; client insisted Kastner be on the project after reading his biography in the proposal.
- **1975** — Brazil foreign exchange crisis impact on IT design: Brazil’s currency controls made importing American computer hardware nearly impossible; online terminal design adapted to paper forms and bicycle couriers.
- **1975** — Social-justice computing definition: Instead of terminals: paper forms; instead of data packets: young men on bicycles pedaling batch-input forms several km from hospital to computer center.
- **1975** — Rio acceptance test environment: Kastner debugged Burroughs code in 104-degree heat; air conditioners stripped from building; notes stained with sweat, ink ran.
- **1975** — Rio customs tape inspection incident: Brazilian customs inspector unspooled magnetic tape reel on floor searching for forbidden images; tape at 800 bpi looks nothing like a photograph.
- **1975** — Technology as vital organ, not whole creature: Rio trip changed how Kastner thought: software is a vital organ; the system is a living organism embedded in a hostile environment—power outages, currency crises, bad roads, bad policy.
- **1975** — Operating environment as limiting factor: The technology was never the limiting factor. The operating environment was.
- **1973** — ADL deployment model: itinerant diagnostician: ADL sent Kastner into paper mills, electric utilities, municipal police departments with expectation he’d understand operations quickly enough to design systems for proposals.
- **1973** — Continental Forest Industries paper mill engagement: Mill produced 2,000-lb kraft paper rolls in continuous industrial ballet; inventory-control system had to decide quickly whether to ship roll or divert to cardboard line.
- **1973** — Real-time inventory decision constraint: If the routing decision took too long, the floor ran out of space; 2,000-lb roll is a one-ton projectile. Software’s job was to be an unseen choreographer.
- **1973** — Lucille Ball Problem analogy: Like the Lucille Ball chocolate-factory sketch—except the chocolates weighed a ton and could kill someone; hesitation in software was fatal.
- **1974** — Wisconsin Electric remote meter reading challenge: Rural service territory connected by party telephone lines and tight capital budgets; dedicated data lines financially impossible.
- **1974** — Five-baud power-line solution: Proposed using power distribution lines as communication medium; low-frequency signals superimposed on 60-Hz current; 5 baud—no new wiring required.
- **1974** — 5-baud vs. modern USB speed comparison: 5 bits per second is roughly one billion times slower than a modern USB connection; bandwidth was not the point—meters needed to report infrequently and honestly.
- **1974** — Appropriate technology principle: Best solution fits physical and economic constraints of actual system, not engineer’s ambitions. Embracing appropriate technology is not settling—it is respecting reality.
- **1975** — Hampton VA police information system transfer assessment: Sent to Hampton, VA to verify whether police information system could be transferred to St. Petersburg, FL; given police radio with officer-needs-assistance code.
- **1975** — Safety warning from Vice Chief of Police: ‘Don’t leave your room at night,’ said the Vice Chief of Police; Kastner spent more mental energy on door lock integrity than database schema.
- **1975** — Police system transferability evaluation: Evaluating Hampton VA police system for transfer to St. Petersburg, FL; assessment included intellectual property verification.
- **1975** — Consequences of specification error in public safety: Police system specification errors would be measured not only in dollars but in officers’ lives if warrant query returned incorrect results.
- **1976** — ADL forensic consulting role evolution: As decade wore on, ADL stopped sending Kastner to design new systems and started sending him as consultant to explain why existing systems were on fire.
- **1976** — Personal journaling practice on troubled engagements: Kept detailed personal journals on every troubled engagement as a black-box recorder; more than once called as witness.
- **1976** — Programmer skills translate to forensic consulting: Paranoia, attention to edge cases, obsession with what logs actually say rather than what they should say translate surprisingly well into forensic consulting work.
- **1977** — Houghton Mifflin system crisis: IT manager fired; new system turned on Jan 1 for accounting convenience; company weeks from being unable to close books; hardware was Honeywell.
- **1977** — IBM tool incompatibility at Honeywell site: Every IBM-era tool and habit Kastner had developed over a decade was suddenly useless on Honeywell hardware.
- **1977** — Root cause of technology project failure: Real danger in tech projects is rarely technical; it is political, managerial, financial misalignment wrapped around mis-scoped, mis-priced, or mis-governed projects.
- **1978** — United Shoe Machinery migration crisis: ADLS management decided to migrate IBM 4300 mainframe code to Data General Eclipse minicomputer; violated architectural physics of both platforms.
- **1978** — United Shoe Machinery system thrashing: System thrashing at every level: CPU, application layer, disks, and boardroom; migration was in production managing real business transactions.
- **1978** — Platform migration implicit assumptions problem: Every application contains thousands of implicit assumptions about hardware (memory addressing, I/O channels, interrupt timing, FP precision) never documented because developers never imagined migration.
- **1978** — Platform migration approved by uninformed management: United Shoe migration was not a software project gone wrong; it was an architectural mismatch approved by people who did not understand the physics.
- **1978** — OEM contract midyear audit: Working with Frank Hawthorne (ADL Systems GE-trained CFO), Kastner conducted midyear audit of firm’s OEM contracts.
- **1978** — ADL Systems OEM earnings inflation discovery: Management had been booking volume discounts as instant profit before the sales volume generating those discounts had been achieved—a classic earnings inflation, pending liability.
- **1978** — Bad accounting kills faster than bad code: Reporting the OEM accounting problem was a defining professional moment; crystallized conviction: bad accounting can kill a company faster than bad code.
- **1974** — Kastner Wang stock options trading: Started trading Wang Laboratories stock options in his early twenties; watched technology market rhythms with increasing attention.
- **1978** — ADL fixed-price business model critique: ADL specialized in fixed-price turnkey contracts when system complexity was exploding beyond reliable scoping; capped upside (fixed fee) while leaving downside open.
- **1978** — Lawsuit risk from fixed-price custom development: ADL was taking equity risk for consulting fees on fixed-price custom development; Kastner could see lawsuits coming.
- **1978** — Minicomputer revolution participants: DEC, Data General, Prime, and a dozen others were selling machines real businesses could operate without a priesthood of systems programmers.
