# Chapter 4: Prime Computer (1979-1981)

> Peter S. Kastner recounts his two years at Prime Computer (1979–1981), covering the company's explosive growth as a minicomputer market leader, its hybrid go-to-market strategy built around the proprietary PRIMOS/INFORMATION platform, and his work in market planning across government and commercial verticals. The chapter traces the arc from Prime's peak stock performance to CEO Ken Fisher’s departure, which Kastner reads as an exit signal, and concludes with the strategic lesson that short-term platform advantages built on proprietary lock-in are ultimately undone when industry economics shift toward open, standardized architectures.

**Author:** Peter S. Kastner · **Date:** 2026-05-14 · **Type:** memoir
**Importance:** high — *This chapter offers a detailed insider account of Prime Computer at its commercial and financial apex, illuminating minicomputer-era sales culture, the Pick/INFORMATION architecture’s competitive positioning, and the structural vulnerabilities that later destroyed the minicomputer segment.*
**Prescience:** high — *Kastner’s identification of proprietary lock-in as a strategic trap and his recognition that the x86/Unix cost curve would overwhelm minicomputer platforms proved accurate within a decade for Prime, DEC, Data General, and HP’s minicomputer lines.*

## Key observations (top 50)

- **1979** — Kastner joins Prime Computer: Arrived at Prime Computer around Thanksgiving 1979, joining Market Planning group.
- **1979** — Physical campus atmosphere at Prime Park: Prime Park on Route 9 in Natick; new glass going up, rental cars crowding visitor spots, headhunters in better suits than engineers.
- **1979** — Prime Computer revenue at Kastner arrival: CEO Ken Fisher had taken Prime from $10 million to a serious growth franchise over six years.
- **1979** — Ken Fisher tenure as Prime CEO: Six years as CEO, growing Prime from $10M hopeful to serious growth franchise by time Kastner arrived.
- **1980** — Prime Computer competitive positioning: By 1980, Prime appeared in the same analyst slide decks as DEC, Data General, and HP as a potential challenger to IBM from below.
- **1980** — Prime Computer stock performance: In 1980, Prime was the number-one moving stock on the New York Stock Exchange, up approximately 272 percent for the year.
- **1980** — Chuck Casale role at Prime and later Aberdeen: Investor Relations VP at Prime; stock up 272% made his job easy; later became Kastner’s partner at Aberdeen Group.
- **1979** — Kastner pre-Prime background contrast: Eight years building systems under fixed-price contracts at ADL, absorbing all risk with capped upside; Prime represented visible equity upside.
- **1980** — Prime go-to-market philosophy: Hybrid of mainframe discipline and Route 128 swagger; named accounts, territorial sales, solution selling around business problems rather than raw MIPS.
- **1980** — Solution-selling concealment of underlying platform: Sold as 'online banking platform' or 'interactive engineering environment'; customer discovered later they bought a Prime 750 running PRIMOS and INFORMATION.
- **1980** — Prime INFORMATION architecture description: Based on Pick OS; used multi-value dynamic array fields; data organized the way business thinks it; no table joins needed.
- **1980** — Pick architecture provenance: Pick OS developed in the 1960s for inventory and distribution systems; used multi-value fields in dynamic arrays vs. relational column-row structure.
- **1980** — INFORMATION strength for commercial applications: Multi-value records mapped naturally to real-world data (variable transactions per account, variable line items per PO) without table joins.
- **1980** — INFORMATION weakness: proprietary lock-in: Every application built on Pick/INFORMATION was captive to Prime hardware; as open systems and relational DBs grew, lock-in became liability not feature.
- **1980** — INFORMATION customer narrative in 1980: Faster application development, tighter integration, no army of COBOL programmers required.
- **1980** — Prime argument against DEC VAX: Responsiveness: shorter application cycles, tighter integration; mid-sized regional bank could look like national institution on modest minicomputer footprint.
- **1980** — Prime VAR/OEM ecosystem: VARs, OEMs, and ISVs building vertical packages on INFORMATION served as force multipliers; 'PR1ME shops' were an exclusive club with own language and lore.
- **1980** — Prime field office admin ratio: One-to-one ratio of sales executives to sales administrators in field offices; far better than most companies; kept paperwork inside legal guardrails at quarter-end.
- **1980** — Compliance contrast: Prime vs. CA and Informix: Unlike Computer Associates and Informix (later Aberdeen clients), no one from Prime ever went to jail for cooking the books.
- **1980** — Informix accounting fraud reference: Named alongside Computer Associates as Aberdeen clients where executives faced legal consequences for revenue manipulation; contrasted with Prime’s compliance.
- **1979** — Schlegel recruits Kastner: Ran Market Planning at Prime; was the brother of an ADL colleague; recruited Kastner and gave him commercial vertical markets, alongside Steve Franson.
- **1979** — Schlegel reassigned to Prime Office launch: By Christmas 1979, Schlegel was given the job of launching Prime Office (office automation) and moved to product planning.
- **1979** — Franson becomes EA to sales VP: Became executive assistant to sales VP Bob Clausen and moved upstairs shortly after Kastner joined.
- **1979** — Kastner left with all commercial customers nominally: After Schlegel and Franson departed their roles, Kastner was nominally left with 'all commercial customers.'
- **1979** — Territory shrinkage from technical reclassification: CAD/CAM at Ford and avionics at General Dynamics reclassified as technical/scientific; Kastner’s commercial client roster shrank accordingly.
- **1979** — Ford as Prime CAD/CAM customer: Ford’s CAD/CAM work at Prime classified as technical/scientific, not commercial; removed from Kastner’s territory.
- **1979** — General Dynamics as Prime avionics customer: Avionics work at General Dynamics classified as technical/scientific; removed from Kastner’s commercial territory.
- **1980** — Lake Tahoe sales kickoff culture: Bob Clausen at craps table, making thousand-dollar bets, with thirty salespeople cheering; followed by product roadmaps, skiing at Heavenly, awards banquet.
- **1980** — Lake Tahoe kickoff as cultural indicator: High confidence, high energy, absolute certainty good times would keep rolling; machine printing money, stock up 272%; no appetite for downside scenarios.
- **1980** — Kastner reaction to Prime sales culture: Coming from eight years at ADL pricing risk on fixed-fee contracts, the culture was exhilarating and slightly alarming in equal measure.
- **1980** — Tarulli wins director role over Kastner: Dick Tarulli, district sales manager from Ohio, beat Kastner for the director’s job; reorganized territory along more conventional vertical lines.
- **1980** — Kastner assigned government vertical market: Government ended up with Kastner after territorial reorganization; turned out to be one of the more interesting assignments of his career.
- **1980** — International government market trips: Government vertical required trips to England, France, Japan, and Australia to understand global markets.
- **1980** — GCHQ Prime system for satellite photo analysis: Kastner visited GCHQ in Cheltenham; a Prime system was doing satellite photo analysis on the Iran-Iraq war.
- **1980** — CIA and MI6 security vetting for GCHQ visit: Kastner vetted by CIA and MI6 prior to GCHQ visit; facility appeared as normal people in normal offices doing anything-but-normal work.
- **1980** — MI6 involvement in GCHQ vetting: Co-vetted Kastner with CIA for GCHQ site access in Cheltenham.
- **1980** — French government market characteristics: In France, government was roughly half the entire economy; every regional Prime opportunity traced back to Paris.
- **1980** — Japanese government market landscape: Local Prime manager in Japan played tour guide through a government landscape dense with ministries and procurement rituals.
- **1980** — Australian dealer TV advertising with Doctor Who: Lionel Singer (Australia) placed Prime Computer in TV commercials alongside the popular Doctor Who TV show; it worked; unprecedented in minicomputer industry.
- **1980** — Minicomputer TV advertising breakthrough: Lionel Singer’s Australian Doctor Who co-promotion was something no one else in the minicomputer industry had managed; successfully put Prime on television.
- **1980** — Reagan defense buildup as tailwind: Reagan defense buildup was creating a steady tailwind for Prime’s government business; Kastner got a New York Times quote during this period.
- **1980** — PROMIS as Prime’s first notable government initiative: PROMIS prosecutor case-management system, developed by INSLAW under federal LEAA grants, caught Prime’s government team attention.
- **1980** — PROMIS system capabilities: Tracked defendants, charges, hearings, evidence, and outcomes in unified database; gave prosecutors complete picture of every case in real time.
- **1980** — DOJ PROMIS deployment contract with Prime hardware: DOJ awarded INSLAW contract to deploy PROMIS across federal prosecutor system; Prime won hardware business for more than twenty systems.
- **1980** — PROMIS later legal controversy: INSLAW later alleged DOJ stole its software and sold it to foreign intelligence agencies; sparked years of congressional investigations and litigation.
- **1980** — PROMIS as early applications generator example: At the time of selling hardware, PROMIS was simply an impressive early example of an applications-generator for information management in public safety.
- **1980** — PROMIS connects to Kastner’s ADL law enforcement work: INSLAW used the same LEAA grant stream that funded ADL’s law enforcement systems work with police departments in Florida and Virginia years earlier.
- **1980** — PROMIS state-level push program: Prime field team launched Kastner’s program to push PROMIS at the state level as well as federal.
- **1980** — New Jersey bid non-compliance incident: Local team delivered only 4 of 7 required proposal copies by deadline; returned after dark, found unlocked window, delivered missing copies to bid room in Trenton.
- **1980** — Corporate counsel explains bid non-compliance limits: Corporate counsel Bernie Bradstreet patiently explained why Prime would not sue New Jersey over a clearly non-compliant bid.
