# Chapter 6: Digital Equipment Corporation — The Mainframe's Last Stand (1987–1988)

> Kastner recounts his 1987–1988 tenure at Digital Equipment Corporation as a Marketing Executive, where he orchestrated the DECtp (DEC Transaction Processing) launch and a covert benchmark of IBM's 3090 mainframe that exposed the 'SNA Tax' — massive hidden overhead in IBM's communications stack. He also describes his pivotal role in founding the Transaction Processing Performance Council (TPC), which established independent, audited, industry-standard OLTP benchmarks. The chapter closes with Kastner's observation that DEC, having beaten the mainframe on price-performance, was itself soon to be surpassed by x86 servers using the very same TPC benchmarks.

**Author:** Peter S. Kastner · **Date:** 2026-05-14 · **Type:** memoir
**Importance:** high — *The chapter documents firsthand the founding of the TPC — a benchmark body whose standards shaped enterprise server procurement for decades — and the covert competitive intelligence operation that exposed the SNA Tax, materially affecting IBM's market position.*
**Prescience:** high — *Kastner's 1987 Yankee Group report predicted mainframe displacement by multiprocessor Intel 386 systems in the early 1990s, which proved accurate; he also foresaw that x86 would displace the VAX architecture DEC was promoting — a prediction confirmed within four years.*

## Key observations (top 50)

- **1987** — Kastner's arrival at DEC: Arrived at DEC in late 1987 carrying intimate technical knowledge from six years at Stratus and a freshly completed ghost-written Yankee Group report.
- **1987** — DEC job title: Title was Marketing Executive — DEC's highest-level individual contributor in marketing.
- **1987** — Kastner's assignment mandate: Assignment from Bill Steul's Corporate Systems group: 'look around DEC for things that needed to be done to improve the enterprise position.'
- **1987** — OLTP as IBM mainframe franchise: Online Transaction Processing was 'still an IBM mainframe franchise' for banking, insurance, retail, and financial services as of 1987.
- **1987** — DEC's OLTP assets: DEC had the VAX architecture, a genuine relational database in Rdb, and a transaction monitor — but lacked a verifiable competitive story.
- **1987** — Yankee Group report publication date: 'The Future of Transaction Processing' published by Yankee Group in January 1987.
- **1987** — Ghost-writing fee for Yankee Group report: Hired by John Logan at Yankee Group for $8,000 and a month of late evenings to ghostwrite 'The Future of Transaction Processing.'
- **1987** — John Logan background: Former Prime Computer colleague who moved to Yankee Group; commissioned Kastner's ghost-written report on transaction processing.
- **1987** — Yankee Group report central prediction: Moore's Law was meeting economics of departmental transaction processing; multiprocessor Intel 386 systems were appearing; mainframe cost-per-transaction advantage was evaporating.
- **1987** — Mainframe displacement timeline prediction: Mainframe displacement was 'not a matter of whether but when — and when was the early 1990s.'
- **1988** — DECtp launch timing: DECtp launch was eight months out when Kastner arrived at DEC; he became apparent Chair of the DECtp Task Force.
- **1987** — DECtp Task Force coordination scope: Kastner networked furiously across ten DEC product groups to build the DECtp story; title of Task Force Chair never received in writing.
- **1988** — DEC OLTP competitive hand: DEC's hand was stronger than team realized: multiprocessor VAX 8200 + transaction-friendly Rdb + acquired OLTP transaction monitor.
- **1988** — SNA Tax concept: The SNA Tax: hidden overhead in IBM's mainframe architecture costing enterprise customers millions of dollars they couldn't see.
- **1988** — DECtp marketing launch budget: DECtp marketing launch budget (excluding product R&D) totaled approximately $2 million — described as 'staggering' for a marketing program in 1988.
- **1988** — Benchmark location: Blind benchmark conducted through a CICS consulting firm in Westwood, Massachusetts using the Cullinet mainframe data center.
- **1988** — John Cullinane's significance: John Cullinane, known to Kastner from PHI days, 'had built one of the first major commercial software companies.' The Route 128 world was always smaller than it appeared.
- **1988** — Blind benchmark objective: Objective: measure IBM's highest possible throughput on a 3090 running DB2, then compare head-to-head against a DEC VAX under identical constraints.
- **1988** — IBM analysts in blind benchmark: IBM's own systems analysts brought in under contract; told in writing IBM was to provide all reasonable services and tuning to maximize throughput; unaware DEC was the client.
- **1988** — Debit-credit benchmark description: Simulates bank account transaction processing; measures TPS with 100 virtual terminals per claimed 1 TPS; requires 10,000 virtual users for 100 TPS claim; response time under one second.
- **1988** — Debit-credit benchmark as industry standard: Called the 'four-minute mile' of enterprise computing; every serious OLTP vendor obsessed with it; single test score could define competitive position for years.
- **1988** — Kastner's debit-credit experience: Kastner had extensive debit-credit benchmark experience from Stratus vs. Tandem competitive engagements.
- **1988** — Westwood benchmark hardware configuration: IBM 3090-400 as System Under Test; IBM 3090-200 as load driver — two of IBM's largest mainframes in the same room, running tests at night and on weekends.
- **1988** — SNA Tax: VTAM session initialization cost: More than one full 3090 CPU-second required to log in a single 3270 terminal via VTAM.
- **1988** — SNA Tax: total session overhead for 100 TPS: 100 TPS benchmark required 10,000 virtual terminals × 1 CPU-second = 10,000 CPU-seconds of session overhead; over an hour of clock time on a 4-processor 3090-400 before a single transaction.
- **1988** — DB2 optimized for query, not transactions: DB2 had been tuned almost exclusively for query workloads; Edgar Codd and Christopher Date designed the relational model for data retrieval; transactional throughput was 'an architectural afterthought.'
- **1988** — Relational model designers: Edgar Codd and Christopher Date are credited as the IBM researchers who invented the relational database model; designed for elegant retrieval, not transactional throughput.
- **1988** — Richard Case's role: DEC head of Competitive Analysis; used an HP color graphing plotter to produce presentation foils from Kastner's benchmark data in under two hours.
- **1988** — VAX vs. IBM 3090 benchmark result: VAX running OLTP database with clean transaction architecture delivered comparable throughput to IBM 3090 at a small fraction of the cost.
- **1988** — Press event presentation strategy: Presented to industry analysts in Boston and press corps in New York; used one color slide showing millions of dollars price difference between IBM 3090-400 and DEC VAX with almost no performance difference.
- **1988** — Benchmark fairness objection anticipated: First reaction in the room: 'That's a sleazy, cheating way for DEC to act.' Kastner held questions to the end; presentation had been built to preempt the objection. Press and analysts accepted it.
- **1988** — DECtp benchmark sales impact: Sales improved measurably following the press and analyst presentation of the DEC vs. IBM benchmark results.
- **1988** — IBM DB2 Version 2 announcement: In October 1988, IBM rushed out a press announcement claiming DB2 now ran much faster than DEC's measurements; DB2 Version 2 announcement was an acknowledgment DEC had found real weaknesses.
- **1988** — Specsmanship phenomenon: By 1988 OLTP benchmark world had degenerated into 'specsmanship' — vendors designed their own tests, optimized every parameter, published results unreproducible in real deployments.
- **1988** — Specsmanship example claims: A vendor could claim 500 TPS by selecting workloads that favored their architecture, excluding real-deployment overhead; every vendor won its own benchmark; customers had no trustworthy comparison basis.
- **1988** — Omri Serlin's advocacy for TPC: Omri Serlin, a respected OLTP analyst, had been pushing for independent audited industry-standard benchmarks for years; industry was hesitant; result was paralysis.
- **1988** — Kastner commits DEC to TPC: Over dinner with Omri Serlin in Los Altos, California: 'Digital Equipment would be pleased to be the first member of the Transaction Processing Council. Let's make some phone calls and get the rest off the fence.'
- **1988** — TPC leak to MIS Week: Kastner leaked Digital's TPC leadership position to MIS Week on August 8, 1988; once DEC's commitment was public, other vendors faced a binary choice: join or explain refusal to compete on a level field.
- **1989** — TPC founding requirements: TPC required full system pricing (hardware + software + maintenance), independent certified audit, and full public disclosure of configuration and methodology.
- **1992** — TPC-C market impact: TPC-C became the definitive OLTP server benchmark through the 1990s and into the 2000s; results published in trade press quarterly and tracked like stock prices in the server market.
- **1989** — TPC transformation of OLTP procurement: TPC transformed the OLTP market: vendors could no longer claim self-serving results; CIOs had objective, auditable data; benchmark became standard in every enterprise server RFP for a decade.
- **1989** — Kastner as TPC auditor: Kastner realized his OLTP technical depth plus TPC methodology made him an accredited benchmark auditor; established Aberdeen Transaction Services as a practice area.
- **1989** — AICPA performance auditing manual: AICPA manual had a brief chapter on performance auditing; Kastner used it as procedural basis; 'the secret was mundane: methodical process and impeccable notes.'
- **1989** — Aberdeen Transaction Services billing rate: Billing rate was $15,000 for a couple of days of careful documentation work per benchmark audit.
- **1989** — Stratus TPC-A audit: Kastner's Aberdeen Transaction Services audited Stratus's TPC-A benchmark submission.
- **1989** — Stratus/IBM RAMP-C audit: Kastner audited the Stratus/IBM proprietary RAMP-C benchmark submission.
- **1990** — Groupe Bull benchmark audits: Kastner audited three models of Groupe Bull DPS 7000 and 6000 systems across the United States, Italy, and France.
- **1990** — European audit accommodations: Kastner conducted one audit session staying at the Trianon Palace Versailles, reviewing benchmark logs at a desk overlooking the palace gardens while engineers wrestled with communications configurations.
- **1993** — ATS auditing practice end: Auditing practice ended with TPC-C, when council began requiring certified auditors with formal examinations under authorized firms. Described as 'a productive and pleasantly remunerative sideline.'
- **1988** — Jack Smith's assessment of DECtp: DEC's senior marketing executive Jack Smith later told peers: 'DECtp was our most important launch after VAX.'
