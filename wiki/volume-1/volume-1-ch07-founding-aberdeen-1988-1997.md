# Chapter 7: Founding Aberdeen Group (1988-1997)

> Peter S. Kastner recounts the founding of Aberdeen Group in 1988 alongside co-founders Chuck Casale, John Logan, and Tom Willmott, describing the firm's deliberate differentiation from the dominant FIGY firms through free-research publishing, deep domain specialization, and a cooperative revenue-sharing model. The chapter traces Aberdeen's analytical frameworks across six technology themes—open architecture, client-server, RDBMS dominance, data as competitive advantage, integration complexity, and the Spoke-Node-Ring model—assessing both prescient calls and missed timing. It concludes with the 1997 inflection point when the partners chose venture capital over remaining a partnership, transforming the firm's structure and culture.

## Key observations (top 50)

- **1989** — Founding recruitment call: Chuck Casale called Kastner at DEC in late 1989 with the proposition to co-found Aberdeen Group.
- **1980** — Casale role at Prime Computer: Casale ran Investor Relations at Prime Computer during its rocket-ship years; stock climbed 272% in a single year.
- **1980** — Stock performance during rocket-ship years: Prime Computer stock climbed 272 percent in a single year during Casale’s tenure in Investor Relations.
- **1989** — Logan’s background and role: Harvard MBA; previously published Kastner’s Yankee Group study on transaction processing; covered financial side of Aberdeen.
- **1989** — Willmott’s background and role: Came from IDC; covered networking and remaining white space not covered by other three founding partners.
- **1988** — Name origin and market testing: \"Aberdeen Group\" scored well in focus-group research as ‘highly trusted, top-notch — but I just can’t place it.’ The firm incorporated in 1988.
- **1990** — Market entry year: Aberdeen entered the enterprise technology research market in 1990, two years after incorporation.
- **1990** — Gartner business model: Gartner (founded 1979) built a subscription model targeting CIOs with vendor evaluations; Magic Quadrant was the industry’s most recognizable analytical tool.
- **1990** — Vendor pressure on Magic Quadrant: Enterprise software vendors coveted Leaders quadrant placement; Gartner analysts received enormous pressure around quarterly reviews.
- **1990** — IDC business model: IDC (founded 1964) dominated market sizing and forecasting with quantitative reports on shipments, revenues, and market share. ‘IDC owned the numbers.’
- **1990** — Example IDC database market sizing claim: To tell a board that the database market was a $4 billion opportunity, you cited IDC.
- **1990** — Forrester business model: Founded 1983 by George Colony; targeted CIOs and business executives; known for Wave reports and rigorous methodology.
- **1990** — Yankee Group focus: Founded 1970; focused on telecommunications and networking; required reading in late 1980s as networking became enterprise-critical.
- **1990** — Differentiation from FIGY firms: Aberdeen built ‘deep technical expertise in specific domains, delivered through concise published research as lead generation for high-margin advisory projects.’ FIGYs sold subscriptions; Aberdeen sold expertise.
- **1990** — Kastner’s coverage domain at Aberdeen: System software, application software, and x86 servers.
- **1990** — Casale’s coverage domain and credential: Scientific and high-performance computing; held registered financial analyst credential giving Aberdeen unique capabilities.
- **1990** — Logan’s coverage domain: Minicomputers.
- **1990** — Willmott’s coverage domain: Networking and everything that didn’t fit the other three buckets.
- **1990** — Revenue-sharing model: selling premium: Partner who originated and closed a deal received a defined percentage; business development explicitly compensated.
- **1990** — Revenue-sharing model: final presentation allocation: Board-level briefings had their own allocation; structured as two-person presentations to ensure quality and expose clients to multiple perspectives.
- **1990** — Revenue model label: Kastner called the structure a ‘cooperative hunter-killer community’ — everyone ate but rainmakers also prospered via selling premiums.
- **1990** — Eat-what-you-kill model assessment: A purely individualist eat-what-you-kill model would have destroyed the firm; research requires review, quality control, and knowledge sharing.
- **1990** — Overhead split model: Office overhead, administration, and rent split equally. Hiring partner assumed full financial risk for analyst salary and allocated overhead.
- **1990** — Analyst margin reward for hiring partner: Approximately 35% margin on analyst-generated revenue accrued to the hiring partner as reward for the hiring risk.
- **1990** — First analyst hire: Kernochan expertise: Deep expertise in database systems and data management; could identify lock-escalation patterns causing performance degradation at scale.
- **1990** — First analyst hire: Ed Black expertise: Covered enterprise applications and business intelligence; became cornerstone of Kastner’s practice expansion alongside Kernochan.
- **1990** — Ideal analyst profile: Former vendor engineers, enterprise architects, systems designers — people who had built and broken things. Ex-reporters also valuable. Analysts could install/configure products they covered.
- **1992** — Analyst base salary (mid-level, early 1990s): $52,000 base salary for mid-level analysts in the early 1990s.
- **1992** — Analyst margin quota: Each analyst carried approximately $350,000 annual margin quota; 30% bonus on overage, uncapped.
- **1992** — Exceptional analyst total compensation range: Exceptional analysts could earn $250,000 to $350,000 in total compensation. Most described it as their best job ever.
- **1990** — Publishing strategy: free research as lead generation: Aberdeen published research freely and used it as lead generation for advisory services; fundamentally different from FIGY subscription models.
- **1993** — Early internet website for free research downloads: Aberdeen launched one of the first research internet websites with free downloads. At peak ranked in top 10 globally per Alexa Internet.
- **1995** — Website research download volume: By 1995, Aberdeen’s website was generating thousands of research downloads weekly, capturing email address and company name for each.
- **1990** — Free-research bet: information wants to be free: Aberdeen bet that in a world where information increasingly wants to be free, expertise and judgment are what clients will pay premium prices for.
- **1990** — Content marketing anticipation: Aberdeen’s free-research distribution model anticipated content marketing by 15 years; deeply contrarian in 1990, conventional wisdom by mid-2000s.
- **1993** — Advisory project margin: Advisory engagements carried 60–70% margins; a single $200,000 advisory project funded substantial research publication.
- **1993** — Inside sales lead qualification via downloads: Inside sales team called companies that had downloaded multiple related reports, knowing they were actively evaluating relevant technology decisions.
- **1990** — Thesis: client-server as permanent architectural principle: Client-server was not a temporary fad; separating presentation, business logic, and data management was correct system design. Technologies would change but logical separation persisted.
- **1990** — Thesis: RDBMS as universal middleware: Relational databases had won the data management battle. ACID transaction guarantees + SQL flexibility = universal standard for operational systems.
- **1990** — Personal RDBMS mission (1985–1995): Personal mission from 1985 to 1995 was championing the RDBMS that could handle both transactions AND query/reporting — the killer combination.
- **1990** — Thesis: open architecture inevitability: Proprietary platforms were economically doomed. Cost advantages of standardized components at volume were overwhelming. Strategic implication: architect for platform replaceability.
- **1995** — Linux analyst hire: Aberdeen hired a Linux analyst as soon as Red Hat took off, reflecting the firm’s open-architecture conviction.
- **1991** — Thesis: data as competitive advantage: Data becoming a strategic asset, not merely an operational record. Enterprises analyzing customer behavior/market trends faster than competitors had sustainable advantages. Kastner has a circa-1991 slide of today’s AI-driven analysis model.
- **1992** — Thesis: integration problem acceleration: As client-server proliferated, integration complexity exploded into N-squared complexity. Pattern: architectural proliferation → integration pain → demand for integration platforms → new standards → further proliferation.
- **1992** — Spoke-Node-Ring framework: S-N-R: road map for integrating heterogeneous computer systems. Systems-of-record at center; middle manager rings run productivity apps on peer-peer networks; productivity nodes on LAN. ‘Lego for building systems architectures.’
- **1990** — Prediction: open systems transition (Unix/x86): Proprietary platforms doomed; Unix and x86 to dominate. Outcome: correct on direction but Unix fragmentation persisted; Linux won via open licensing not technical standardization.
- **1990** — Prediction: client-server persistence (outcome): Architectural principle persisted across implementation generations: terminals → PCs → browsers → mobile. Web apps, mobile apps, cloud services all follow client-server patterns.
- **1990** — Prediction: RDBMS dominance (outcome): Relational databases remained dominant for operational systems through the 2010s. NoSQL carved out specific use cases but never displaced relational for enterprise applications.
- **1991** — Prediction: data warehousing democratization (outcome): Every significant enterprise eventually built data warehouses. Technologies evolved Teradata → columnar stores → cloud warehouses, but architectural pattern persisted.
- **1992** — Prediction: integration as first-class problem (outcome): Integration complexity accelerated as predicted, driving massive investment in middleware, EAI, SOA, and eventually API management. Clients who acted on early Aberdeen research avoided enormous technical debt.
