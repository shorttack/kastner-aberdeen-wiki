# Chapter 8: The Go-Go Years — Aberdeen at Scale (1998–2006)

> Peter S. Kastner chronicles Aberdeen Group's transformation from a boutique partnership into a venture-backed research firm employing up to 150 analysts during the internet boom, and its subsequent collapse and recovery following the dot-com crash. The chapter details audited revenue and profit figures from 1997 to 2006, the firm's methodological evolution (benchmark research, TCO models, best-practices frameworks), key analytical predictions (SOA, open-source enterprise adoption, consumerization of IT), the CEO transition to Jamie Bedard, and the October 2006 acquisition by Harte-Hanks for $42 million just before the financial crisis.

His framing of 'data integration as perpetual tax' remains valid as of 2026.*

## Key observations (top 50)

- **1998** — Aberdeen venture capital transformation trigger: Venture capital infusion transformed Aberdeen from partnership to growth-mandated corporation; question shifted from sustainable profitability to exit speed.
- **1997** — Analyst headcount 1997: ~30 analysts (partnership model)
- **2000** — Analyst headcount 2000: ~120 analysts (peak of internet boom)

- **2006** — Analyst headcount 2006 (pre-acquisition): ~19 analysts (recovered, positioned for exit)

- **2001** — Net profit/(loss) 2001: ($3,233,819)

- **2002** — Net profit/(loss) 2002: ($1,340,556)

- **2003** — Net profit/(loss) 2003: ($3,064,707)
- **2004** — Revenue 2004 (trough year): $5,107,002
- **2004** — Net profit/(loss) 2004: ($2,017,318)
- **2005** — Revenue 2005: $8,652,215
- **2005** — Net profit/(loss) 2005: $868,498
- **2006** — Revenue Jan-Aug 2006: $10,828,756
- **2006** — Net profit Jan-Aug 2006: $3,971,626
- **2000** — Revenue growth 1997 to 2000: Nearly three-fold growth from $7.2M (1997) to $20.7M (2000)
- **2004** — Revenue decline 2000-2004: 75 percent decline from $20.7M (2000) to $5.1M (2004) over four years
- **2004** — Accumulated net losses 2001-2004: More than $9.6 million in accumulated net losses from 2001 through 2004
- **2006** — 2006 net profit margin: Nearly 37 percent net profit margin on Jan-Aug 2006 revenue
- **1998** — Partnership compensation model replaced: Multi-layered cooperative revenue-sharing replaced by market-rate salaries and conventional bonus plans; model could not scale beyond ~12 analysts
- **1998** — Revenue sharing complexity limit: Cannot administer complex revenue splits across 100 analysts; feasible across 12.
- **1998** — New coverage domains at scale: Supply chain management, CRM, human capital management, vertical industry analysis (financial services, telecom, retail, manufacturing, healthcare)
- **2003** — Kastner role shift 2003-2006: Board asked Kastner to step aside from 2003 to 2006 while Bedard restructured business model; Kastner kept Intel as retainer
- **2006** — Kastner returned to Aberdeen 2006 for SOA research: Returned in 2006 to drive SOA research, contributing to 2005-2006 revenue and profitability recovery
- **1999** — Benchmark research program launch: Systematic surveys of hundreds of enterprises on technology adoption patterns, deployment challenges, and satisfaction metrics
- **2000** — Benchmark report credibility signals: CIOs used Aberdeen benchmarks to justify budget decisions; vendors quoted them in sales materials and challenged assumptions when numbers didn't favor them.
- **1999** — Best practices frameworks development: Captured architectural decisions, org structures, vendor selections, and PM approaches from successful deployments as reusable frameworks
- **1999** — TCO modeling as signature commercial tool: TCO calculators for major technology categories became Aberdeen's most commercially potent tool; incorporated hardware, software, implementation, maintenance, training over 3-5 year horizons
- **1999** — TCO model analytical positioning: TCO models positioned Aberdeen's advisory work as quantitatively rigorous rather than qualitatively opinionated; both vendor challenge and endorsement proved the models were landing.
- **1999** — TCO vs sticker price procurement example: Platform $1M cheaper to license might cost $3M more to implement, $2M more to staff, $1.5M more to maintain: net $5.5M more expensive over 5 years.
- **2000** — TCO model limitation: assumption dependence: TCO models only as good as their assumptions; vendor disagreements about TCO outcomes were usually disagreements about specific assumptions, creating valuable client conversations.
- **1998** — Internet bubble demand for analyst validation: Every internet startup and every enterprise digital initiative needed analyst validation; research market demand was 'irrational in the best possible direction'
- **2000** — Aberdeen team knew bubble was unsustainable: Analyst team made dark jokes about which internet business models would survive; told clients directly when asked. Not everyone asked.
- **2000** — Bubble timing impossibility: Knowing a bubble exists doesn't mean you can time its deflation.
- **2000** — NASDAQ peak date: NASDAQ peaked in March 2000; Aberdeen revenue peaked same year at $20.7 million
- **2002** — 9/11 aftermath impact on revenue: Revenue fell to $9.4M in 2002, hurt by the 9/11 attack aftermath
- **2003** — Analyst headcount reduction post-crash: Headcount cut from ~150 analysts in 2001 to ~40 by 2003
- **2002** — Benchmark research collapse during downturn: Survey-based benchmark research became less useful when response rates collapsed; companies in survival mode don't have time for analyst surveys.
- **2002** — Deep technical advisory durability in downturn: Deep technical advisory work held up during downturn; enterprises still needed complex integration, architecture, and vendor selection help even with constrained budgets.
