# Chapter 9: Expert Witness — Technology on Trial (1992–2015)

> Peter Kastner recounts a parallel career as a technology expert witness spanning 1992–2015, alongside his Aberdeen Group research practice. Key engagements include a major data-warehousing antitrust dispute involving Safeway and a Teradata machine, software project failure forensics, and a Florida statewide welfare-system bid protest in which Kastner’s IBM performance analysis was later validated by the project’s real-world failure. The chapter distills methodological lessons: governance failure almost always precedes technical failure, market-research frameworks map directly onto litigation analysis, and adversarial expert scrutiny instills a permanent standard of rigor.

## Key observations (top 50)

- **1992** — Expert witness career initiation: Received unsolicited call from unknown law firm seeking expert on enterprise database technology; said yes, opening a parallel career spanning 20+ years.
- **1992** — First case type: Complex commercial litigation involving enterprise database technology; required expertise in relational databases at architectural and market level.
- **1992** — Parallel career duration: Expert witness practice ran alongside Aberdeen research practice for more than two decades (1992–2015).
- **1992** — Consulting vs. testifying expert distinction: Consulting expert: confidential, candid, not disclosed. Testifying expert: formal opinions, depositions, cross-examination — every opinion must be bulletproof.
- **1992** — Routes into technology expert witness work in 1990s: Three routes: former senior engineers, former CIOs, and technology analysts with research credentials. Analyst credential proved advantageous.
- **1992** — Aberdeen credentials accepted by courts: Aberdeen’s published research and TPC work gave Kastner credentials courts found credible for technology expert testimony.
- **1992** — Analyst advantage over engineer in litigation: As analyst (not engineer), Kastner could speak to what industry knew, what market expected, and what competent technology practice looked like — the standards by which decisions are judged.
- **1992** — Case subject matter range: Cases ranged from database licensing disputes to software project failures to antitrust claims in the storage market.
- **1995** — Safeway litigation characterization: Most significant litigation engagement of Kastner’s expert witness career: major antitrust and breach-of-contract dispute.
- **1995** — Safeway dispute core issues: Centered on data warehousing technology: what had been promised, what had been delivered, whether competitive market analysis informing the deal was sound, and damages.
- **1995** — Kastner role in Safeway case: Second-seat to novice expert Hugh Bishop in the Safeway engagement.
- **1995** — Data warehouses as strategic retail infrastructure: By mid-1990s, data warehouses had become strategic infrastructure for large retailers, consolidating POS, supplier, and inventory data for analytical decision-making.
- **1995** — Safeway competitive implications of data warehouse: Better analytical capability translated directly into better purchasing negotiations, more effective promotions, and reduced inventory costs — all affecting margins in a thin-margin business.
- **1995** — Safeway Teradata machine destruction: Safeway’s Teradata data warehouse machine was effectively destroyed when the fire extinguishing system exploded into the closed computer room.
- **1995** — Kastner damages expert role in Safeway: Role was damages expert: built analytical model to answer what market value of contracted technology was, how it differed from delivery, and economic consequence of that gap.
- **1995** — Aberdeen frameworks applied in Safeway litigation: Drew on Aberdeen’s competitive landscape analysis, TCO models, and performance benchmarking methodology; research and litigation used the same analytical tools in different contexts.
- **1998** — Safeway engagement duration: Ran over three years, involved dozens of depositions, produced hundreds of pages of expert reports.
- **1998** — Safeway case outcome: Settlement terms confidential; technical and economic analysis admitted into evidence; established market value of enterprise data warehousing in terms courts could understand.
- **1998** — Expert testimony contribution in technology disputes: Case established market value of enterprise data warehousing capabilities in terms courts could understand and apply — described as the fundamental contribution of expert testimony.
- **1995** — Data warehouse damages quantifiability: When a data warehouse project failed or underperformed, damages were calculable but only with expert analysis quantifying properly functioning vs. failed system performance.
- **1995** — Software project failure pattern — scope never locked: Initial contracts described systems at a level of abstraction both parties could agree to but left implementation details undefined; client and vendor diverged on what the system should do.
- **1995** — Software project failure pattern — requirements changed faster than contracts: Business environments change; ERP implementations starting 1999 encountered Y2K remediation priorities in 1998; change orders introduced adversarial dynamics.
- **1995** — Software project failure pattern — integration underspecified: New system always had to connect to existing systems never adequately described in the contract; integration scope was the site of the most damaging disputes.
- **1995** — Software project failure pattern — testing inadequate: User acceptance testing frequently rushed to meet contractual go-live dates; defects discovered in production were far more expensive to remediate.
- **2010** — Standish CHAOS Report IT project success rates: Roughly 30% succeed as defined; 50% succeed partially; 20% fail completely. Rates have improved modestly with agile, cloud, SaaS but persist over 30 years.
- **2010** — Root cause of IT project failures: Technical failure was real but not root cause; root cause was governance failure: inadequate requirements, insufficient change management, misaligned incentives, or executive override.
- **2015** — Genuine technical incompetence vs. governance failure frequency: In 20 years of expert witness work on software project failures, encountered genuine technical incompetence perhaps 5 times; encountered governance and organizational failures in every single case.
- **1995** — Expert report questions in software project failures: Three questions: (1) What did the contract actually require? (2) What was actually delivered? (3) What were the consequences?
- **1995** — Contract interpretation standard in technology disputes: Contracts frequently ambiguous; question is what an objective, reasonable reader would understand given industry standards and practices.
- **1995** — Damages categories in failed IT projects: Include: cost of work paid for but not received; cost of remediation; operational losses during underperformance period; competitive disadvantage from market position loss.
- **1995** — Aberdeen market research value in damages analysis: Quantifying competitive consequences of technology failure required understanding what properly functioning technology would have enabled — exactly the comparative analysis Aberdeen performed for advisory clients.
- **1992** — ADL forensic methodology transfer to expert witness work: Forensic habits from ADL — detailed records, spotting scope creep and governance failures, reading a project log like a doctor reads a chart — proved directly applicable.
- **1992** — Florida welfare bid overview: EDS and IBM bid on $200M statewide Florida human services application; RFP required specific terminal response times on average.
- **1992** — Unisys retains Kastner for Florida bid protest: Unisys, a losing bidder, contacted Kastner to serve as expert witness to prove IBM’s three-tier mainframe system could not meet RFP specifications.
- **1992** — IBM performance documentation in Florida case: IBM delivered a box of computer paper with 525 performance simulation runs and nothing else.
- **1992** — Kastner finds needle in haystack in IBM performance data: Found critical data point in 525-run IBM simulation; testified IBM’s own performance estimator showed the system would have 20–30 second average response times.
- **1992** — IBM Florida welfare system RFP response time issue: IBM’s own performance estimator indicated 20–30 second average response times on a system whose RFP required specific lower thresholds.
- **1992** — Florida bid protest outcome: The protest lost; Kastner returned to his day-to-day work.
- **1995** — Florida welfare system actual cost and failure: System was built at $200M cost; failed acceptance tests with 20–30 second response times — exactly as Kastner had predicted.
- **1995** — Florida welfare bid corruption: Several state officials were indicted over the bid after the system’s failure.
- **1996** — Congressional response to Florida welfare system failure: Congress held hearings and passed two laws preventing unfettered contracts like Florida’s; Congress had put up most of the lost millions.
- **1996** — Kastner vindication in Florida case: Kastner notes he was vindicated when the system failed exactly as his analysis predicted, despite losing the original bid protest.
- **2026** — Florida case documentation gap: Kastner notes the Florida account is incomplete; needs to flesh out with more factual data from state archives while preparing memoirs.
- **2015** — Expert witness vantage point on technology markets: Courts see markets through transactions that failed; most market participants experience markets through transactions that succeeded. Expert witness work provides the failure-side perspective.
- **2015** — Contracts describe aspirations not obligations: Enterprise technology contracts are frequently written in language both parties understand differently; ambiguity is sometimes how deals get done; cost paid at implementation or in court.
- **2015** — Technical credibility as scarcest resource in technology disputes: Attorneys understand legal concepts deeply and technology superficially; tech experts vice versa; value of an expert understanding both dimensions is disproportionate to their fee.
- **2015** — Organizational failure precedes technical failure: In software project disputes, the technical problems that ended up in court were almost never the first sign of trouble; before code was bad, governance was bad; before system failed, communication had failed.
- **2015** — Market research and litigation analysis use the same tools: Aberdeen’s analytical frameworks — market definition, competitive comparison, TCO modeling, performance benchmarking — were the same frameworks courts needed. Translation was only methodological.
- **2015** — Research vs. litigation presentation of analysis: Research conclusions presented with confidence levels; legal opinions presented with certainty. The underlying analysis is identical.
- **2015** — Expert witness practice end date: Active expert witness practice wound down around 2015, concurrent with transition away from independent consulting work following Aberdeen departure.
